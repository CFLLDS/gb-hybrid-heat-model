# Flexibility markets, the 2030 target and what households are paid

*Literature strand for "Hiding the wiring again". Sources accessed 7 November 2026. Full quotes, locators and references are in `flex_markets.json`. This strand picks up where `controls_flex.md` stops, after the 2022/23 and 2023/24 Demand Flexibility Service (DFS) winters.*

## 1. How much flexibility Great Britain wants by 2030

The government's targets come in three layers, and they are easily conflated. The broadest is the Clean Flexibility Roadmap's restatement of the Clean Power 2030 Action Plan. It sets a total of 51–66 GW of clean flexibility by 2030, "a two to three-fold increase on the 24 GW of capacity installed in 2023" (DESNZ, Ofgem and NESO 2025). Within that total, the Action Plan itself describes an *increase* of 29–35 GW in short-duration flexibility from 2023 levels, spread "across battery storage, consumer-led flexibility and interconnection capacity" (DESNZ 2024). The Roadmap restates this increment as 28–36 GW (DESNZ, Ofgem and NESO 2025). The Action Plan should be treated as the authoritative source. The narrowest layer is consumer-led flexibility (CLF): 10–12 GW by 2030, excluding storage heaters, against 2.5 GW utilised in 2023 (DESNZ 2024). The Action Plan expects electric-vehicle smart charging to drive that growth.

Hybrid heat pumps barely appear in these numbers. In Table 1 of the Roadmap, NESO's Holistic Transition scenario gives hybrid heat pump flexibility as 0.0 GW in 2024, 0.0 GW in 2030 and 0.2 GW in 2050 (DESNZ, Ofgem and NESO 2025). Delivery so far has also been modest. By July 2026, NESO reported only 170 MW of *new* CLF dispatchable in its own markets, the Balancing Mechanism (BM) and DFS (DESNZ, Ofgem and NESO 2026). Its industrial and commercial target is 750 MW by 2030. The 10–12 GW ambition therefore depends largely on implicit flexibility, meaning time-of-use tariffs under half-hourly settlement. More than 11.3 million meters had migrated to half-hourly settlement by mid-June 2026 (DESNZ, Ofgem and NESO 2026). Explicitly traded volume is a much smaller part of the total.

## 2. The DFS after 2024: from scarcity payment to spot market

Winter margins were forecast to be adequate in 2024/25, so NESO turned DFS from a winter contingency service into "a year-round merit-based margin tool", live from 27 November 2024 (NESO 2025b). The commercial change was large. In 2023/24 the average accepted bid was £1,110.9/MWh (ESO 2024). In 2024/25, NESO published 56 Service Requirements and procured volume on 44 of them. It accepted 5,449.6 MWh, and 3,917.7 MWh was delivered, a delivery rate of 71.9% (NESO 2025a). The average accepted price was £210/MWh, ranging from £59 to £1,290/MWh. The highest price came on 8 January 2025 between 18:00 and 19:00. Settled spend was £943,983 (NESO 2025a). Day-ahead procurement was removed, so DFS is now a within-day product. A Guaranteed Acceptance Price (GAP) remained in the terms, but "neither were issued during the winter period" (NESO 2025a).

A full NESO review of 2025/26 was not found (see `not_verified`). The evidence that is available shows a cheaper and smaller market. Across the merit-based period up to February 2026 there were 148 live events, with volume procured on 117 occasions (NESO 2026c). In the fourth quarter of 2025, £274,000 was accepted across 35 events for 1,721 MWh. The average price was £152/MWh and the maximum £350/MWh (Drax 2026). From April 2026 the service became zonal, with a 0.1 MW minimum bid, and it is now being used for constraint management (NESO 2026a; 2026d; 2026e). A GAP has since been used only for tests: £500/MWh for turn-down and £180/MWh for turn-up in August–September 2026. NESO set these prices "in line with the high end of attainable revenue" (NESO 2026d). Domestic participation is still small and manual. Of domestic delivery, 91% came in steps below 1 kW, and more than 99% of MPANs were manually initiated (NESO 2025a).

## 3. How the baseline works

The baseline matters most for this article. For domestic sites, DFS uses Elexon's BSC P376 method BL01 "without the final in day adjustment" (NESO 2026a). The method works as follows:

1. Search the 60 days before the event for eligible days of the same type.
2. Exclude Event Days. These include days with a DFS acceptance, a DNO flexibility instruction or Capacity Market delivery.
3. On a working day, take the ten most recent eligible days.
4. On a non-working day, take the four most recent and keep the middle two by daily volume.
5. For each settlement period, average the metered half-hours across the selected days (NESO 2026a).

NESO retained this method in 2025/26 because no alternative was shown to be more accurate at portfolio level (NESO 2025b). Respondents to its consultation nonetheless called it "potentially imperfect and open to gaming" (NESO 2026b). DNO markets use the same structure for "recent history" baselines: ten workdays or four non-workdays (UK Power Networks 2025). DNO markets also allow other baselines. Storage uses a *zero* baseline, so every exported kilowatt-hour counts as delivery. Domestic heat pumps are measured against a *fixed* deemed profile, with a maximum turn-down of 2 kW in winter (UK Power Networks 2025). Elexon, appointed market facilitator in July 2024 and live since December 2025, is standardising these baselines into five defined methods (Elexon 2024; 2025; 2026; Ofgem 2025).

## 4. What households are actually paid

Axle Energy's offer is verified at source: "£1 per kWh you export during grid events, with a minimum of £10 a month" (Axle Energy 2026a). That is equivalent to £1,000/MWh. Typical homes see three to six one-hour events a month, and most events are "called by a regional network for its own area", with DFS events at shorter notice (Axle Energy 2026b; 2026c). Three qualifications matter:

- **It pays for battery export, not demand reduction.** Members join through a supported battery inverter, so a household with a heat pump but no battery cannot take part (Axle Energy 2026a).
- **The terms are weaker than the marketing.** "Rewards are not guaranteed and are subject to change". Some programmes measure delivery "against a baseline using data from your smart meter". Axle pays only from revenue it receives from programme operators (Axle Energy 2026d).
- **The public prices are lower.** Clearing prices in the public record are well below £1,000/MWh: a DFS average of £152–210/MWh, a DFS test GAP of £500/MWh and a historical UK Power Networks ceiling of "up to £600/MWh" (NESO 2025a; Drax 2026; NESO 2026d; UK Power Networks 2026). The fetched sources do not explain how Axle funds the difference. No source verified Axle as a Virtual Lead Party in the BM. The BM still requires bids of at least 1 MW in whole megawatts (NESO 2026e), so small domestic assets can enter it only as part of an aggregation.

## 5. The "nothing left to sell" problem for a price-aware hybrid

A hybrid heat pump under software-defined, price-aware control already avoids electricity at the evening peak. It switches to gas, or coasts, whenever peak import is dearer than gas divided by boiler efficiency. Under P376 that behaviour is recorded in the baseline itself. The ten preceding non-event working days already show near-zero heat-pump load from 16:00 to 19:00, so a DFS event finds nothing to turn down. The flexibility has already been used every day through the tariff and cannot be sold a second time as explicit turn-down. This leads to a perverse result: a badly controlled hybrid, running electrically at peak on ordinary days, would earn more on event days than a well-controlled one. NESO's own observation that frequent events made baselining "challenging" shows how fragile the method is (NESO 2025a).

Under a deemed heat-pump profile of the kind UK Power Networks uses, a hybrid already on gas would, on a plain reading, *appear* to deliver up to 2 kW. That would reward the default state rather than any change in behaviour. Whether a DNO accepts such delivery from hybrids is unresolved and should be confirmed before any claim is made. The household's realistic route to Axle-style payments is a battery, which is separate from the heating system. Two conclusions follow for the argument. First, a hybrid's main contribution to the 10–12 GW is implicit, delivered through the tariff, and is largely invisible to explicit markets. Second, every event-day switch to gas carries a carbon cost, which the carbon strand addresses.
