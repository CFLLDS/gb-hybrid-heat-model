## Abstract

British policy has written off the hybrid heat pump, which keeps the gas boiler, on modelling that assumed it burned gas only at the peak, while installed hybrids switch on a fixed outdoor temperature or on prices the householder enters. This paper models the control. __HOMES_PER_TOWN__ homes in each of __TOWNS__ British towns, calibrated to __HPM_SYSTEMS__ measured heat pumps, are simulated half-hourly through all __N_YEARS__ heating years from __FIRST_YEAR__ to __LAST_YEAR__ at 2025–26 tariffs, under a fixed changeover, a price-aware controller (C-COP) and software-defined power (SD-Power) dispatch, which also holds Britain's demand under a cap.

In this design, with Intelligent Octopus Go among the four tariffs, the tariff explains __VAR_TARIFF_PCT__% of the variation in whether keeping the boiler lowers running costs (__VAR_TARIFF_NOIOG_PCT__% without it). Keeping it means keeping the gas standing charge, about £__GAS_SC_GBP_YEAR__ a year, so a hybrid is cheaper only where it saves more than that in energy. In the median winter it does so for __HYB_WINS_SVR_MEDIAN__% of homes on a flat tariff, and for __HYB_WINS_IOG_MEDIAN__% on Intelligent Octopus Go, whose daytime rate an all-electric home without pre-heating cannot avoid (__REV_PRE_HYB_IOG__% when both homes pre-heat). On Agile, the one tariff priced from the wholesale market, the answer depends on how historic weather is paired with today's market: over twelve test winters the hybrid wins __REV4_WM_HYB_CAL_AGILE__% of homes when each day takes the prices of the same date in 2025–26, and __REV4_WM_HYB_AGILE__% when it takes those of a recent day as cold. On a flat tariff the answer turns on commissioning: over twelve test winters it is __REV_COP_MID_HYB_SVR__% under the central assumption, __REV_COP_HI_HYB_SVR__% for a well-tuned stock and __REV_COP_LO_HYB_SVR__% at the field-trial median. Price-aware control saves £__SAVE_BIV0_SVR_MEAN__–£__SAVE_BIV0_IOG_MEAN__ a year against a fixed switch at 0 °C; on a flat tariff it emits __KG_GAP_CCOP_BIV0_SVR__ kg of CO₂ a year more than that switch, and __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_NOW_SVR__ kg more than an all-electric heat pump.

On a flat tariff a hybrid stock removes __PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_SVR__% of the winter peak that all-electric heating would add. On dynamic tariffs it herds into the same cheap half-hours and removes __PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_COSY__–__PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_IOG__%, in a stress case where homes act together far more than real ones do; letting the herd raise prices, in a simple feedback experiment, shrinks it without removing it. Idealised SD-Power dispatch holds Britain's demand at the flat-tariff hybrid's own peak, so by construction it removes the whole Agile herd above that cap: __HERD_EXCESS_AGILE_GW__ GW in the worst of __N_YEARS__ winters, or, over twelve test winters with 8,000 homes a town, __REV_DSDP_B_AGILE_IDEAL_GW__ GW in the main model and __REV_DSDP_D_AGILE_IDEAL_GW__ GW with heating habits measured in real homes. It costs about £__SDP_COST_AGILE_MEAN__ a home a year in fuel, before delivery costs. Over twelve test winters, a cap on demand net of wind sends homes to gas less often, for about £__REV4_RESID_COST_AGILE__ instead of £__REV4_GROSS_COST_AGILE__, but lets national demand rise to __REV4_RESID_GROSS_PEAK_AGILE__ GW instead of __REV4_GROSS_GROSS_PEAK_AGILE__. With realistic forecast errors or imperfect scheduling it still takes off __REV_SDP_AGILE_FC_LARGE_AR_GW__–__REV_SDP_AGILE_SCHEDULED_REAL_GW__ GW but breaches the cap in every winter; reactive dispatch keeps little. At each winter's own prices since 1961, the hybrid's heating bill was below the all-electric heat pump's in __BURDEN_P10_CCOP_LOWEST_YEARS__ of __BURDEN_P10_YEARS__ winters; by construction it is never above gas alone.

The results do not make a general case for hybrids: under the central assumptions the all-electric heat pump is the cheaper to run for most homes on a flat tariff, and for almost all where well commissioned. They show that the choice of heat source interacts with heat-pump efficiency, tariff design, carbon and the national peak, and that control must be judged separately from the heating it controls. The licence for home energy controllers should set what a controller may optimise, not only who may hold it.

## 1. Introduction: the wiring is back

In a short film that still circulates among energy people, an Olympic track cyclist tries to power a domestic toaster. He is one of the strongest sprinters of his generation. The toaster needs about 700 watts. By the end he is spent and the bread is barely brown [@Grossman2015Toaster]. The point of the film is not the cyclist. It is how completely the electricity system has hidden from us the work it does. For a century, in a phrase of the author's own [@Lewis2026Olympian], the engineers who built Britain's grid "hid the wiring": they made supply so large, so central and so reliable that a household never had to think about where its power came from, when, or at what cost.

Electrification is pulling the wiring back into the home. A household that replaces its gas boiler with a heat pump, adds a battery, takes a half-hourly tariff and puts panels on the roof has, without meaning to, become a systems integrator. It now owns decisions the grid used to make for it: which source of heat to use, when to store energy and when to spend it, and how to trade comfort against cost against carbon, every half-hour. Most households are not equipped to make those decisions and should not have to be. This paper is about the software that could make them instead, and about one specific decision where that software matters more than anywhere else: whether to keep the gas boiler.

### The hybrid question

A hybrid heating system pairs an air-source heat pump with a gas boiler, usually the one already on the wall. British policy has moved firmly against it. The Climate Change Committee's Seventh Carbon Budget does not consider fossil-fuel hybrids at all [@CCC2025_7CB, p. 164]; the National Energy System Operator's 2025 Future Energy Scenarios state that natural-gas hybrids are "no longer installed, as they are not net zero compliant" [@NESO2025_FES_Pathways, p. 103]; and the Boiler Upgrade Scheme will not fund any system that combines a heat pump with fossil-fuel heating [@Ofgem_BUS_installers]. Yet the same scenarios still have roughly 19.5 million homes with a gas boiler in 2035 in every net-zero pathway (19.4–19.6 million) [@NESO2025_FES_Workbook, sheet F.45]. The boiler is not leaving most British homes this decade. The practical question is what it should be allowed to do while it is there.

The case for the hybrid has always rested on an assumption about its control. In the Sixth Carbon Budget the CCC assumed that hybrids "can operate in heat pump mode up to 80% of the time" and found that at 50% they become "significantly less cost effective" [@CCC2020_6CB_Buildings, p. 29]. That 80% was an input, fixed outside the model. It was not the result of any controller responding to prices, weather or the state of the grid. The whole-system studies that found hybrids the cheapest route to decarbonised heat assumed near-ideal dispatch: little gas off-peak, all gas at the peak [@Strbac2018]. The largest recent field trial found something different. In the Electrification of Heat demonstration the heat pumps inside hybrids supplied a median of only 40% of space heating, at a median efficiency of 2.50, and their switch to the boiler "generally corresponded to a certain external temperature" [@ESC2024]. Manufacturers' controllers offer more than that, but not much more. Vaillant's hybrid manager can follow a tariff, yet its factory setting is a fixed outdoor changeover temperature [@Vaillant2024Top10]. Daikin's integrated hybrid, set to its economical mode, chooses the cheaper source in all conditions, but from prices the householder types in and must re-enter after every tariff change, with up to three electricity price levels on a timer; paired with another maker's boiler, it falls back to a temperature rule [@Daikin2026IRG; @DaikinUK2021UG]. What none of them does is follow a price that changes every half-hour, keep its prices current without the householder, weigh carbon, or act with other homes.

The gap between the hybrid that was modelled and the hybrid that was installed is a gap in software. That is the subject of this paper.

### Software-defined power

I use the term **SD-Power**, a working name for the next stage of LDS's Levant platform [@Lewis2026Olympian], for a software layer that hides the wiring again. It borrows its structure from software-defined wide-area networking (SD-WAN), which solved a similar problem for corporate networks fifteen years ago. SD-WAN is defined as "an overlay connectivity service that optimizes transport of IP Packets over one or more Underlay Connectivity Services by recognizing applications … and determining forwarding behavior by applying Policies to them" [@MEF2021_70_1, s. 3]. It did not make cheap internet links better. It made them usable, by putting a controller in charge of which link carried which traffic [@Lewis2026Olympian].

Mapped onto a home, SD-Power has three layers:

- **the underlay**: the physical home and its connections: meter, tariff, heat pump, boiler, battery, and the thermal mass of the building itself;
- **the overlay**: a description of each home's flexibility, expressed the same way whoever made the equipment;
- **the controller**: one place where the decision is made, each half-hour, about which source serves which demand.

The analogy breaks in one important place. SD-WAN puts applications first; SD-Power must put people first. Comfort is the floor, not the variable [@Lewis2026Olympian]. The controller may choose how heat is made, never whether the house is warm. Its success is measured not in raw efficiency but in what I have called the *commercial coefficient of performance* (C-COP): useful heat per penny, relative to what the same heat would cost from gas.

A hybrid is the simplest possible SD-Power system: two underlays (a heat pump and a boiler), one demand (heat), and a single decision to make each half-hour. If software cannot add value there, it is unlikely to add value anywhere in the home. If it can, the question is how much, for whom, in which weather, and at what cost to the climate and the grid.

### What this paper does

This paper builds a national model of that decision and runs it through every heating year in the modern weather record. It simulates __HOMES_PER_TOWN__ sampled homes in each of __TOWNS__ towns from Penzance to Wick, half-hour by half-hour, through __N_YEARS__ real heating years from __FIRST_YEAR__ to __LAST_YEAR__: __HOUSEDAYS_TOTAL__ house-days and __DECISIONS_TOTAL__ heating decisions per tariff. Every simulated day is priced on the actual regional half-hourly tariffs and grid carbon intensity of the same calendar day in 2025–26. The heat pumps are calibrated to __HPM_SYSTEMS__ measured British systems on heatpumpmonitor.org and bounded below by the national field-trial median. One monitored house, my own in Poole, anchors the calibration and serves as a case study.

The model compares the controllers a hybrid could run: the fixed outdoor-temperature switch that is the factory setting of many hybrids today, a price-aware C-COP controller, a carbon-aware variant that adds a price on CO₂, and an SD-Power dispatch that also keeps Britain's total electricity demand under a cap by sending a share of homes to gas when the system is stretched. It reports what each does to the household bill, to emissions under average and marginal grid carbon, and to the winter electricity peak scaled to Britain's gas-heated homes.

The contribution is threefold. First, it replaces the assumed hybrid of the policy literature with a controlled one, and measures the difference. Second, it turns one or two "cold years" into a distribution of __N_YEARS__, so that a hard winter can be ranked against every winter Britain has had since 1940, while recognising that a warming record is not a set of draws from today's climate. Third, it reports the household's bill, the climate's carbon and the grid's peak for the *same* control rule, which is the test the whole-system literature has assumed but not carried out.

Section 3 states the research questions and hypotheses, which were written down before the national results existed. Section 4 describes the model and how it was validated. Section 5 presents the results, hypothesis by hypothesis, with the sensitivity runs and a historical fuel-poverty analysis. Section 6 discusses what they mean for prices, regulation, flexibility markets and fuel poverty; Section 7 sets out the limitations; Section 8 concludes.
