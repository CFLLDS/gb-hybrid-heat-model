## 2. What we know, and what we do not

The evidence bearing on a controlled hybrid comes from four literatures that rarely cite each other: studies of peak electricity demand from electrified heat, field trials of hybrid systems, the carbon accounting used in policy, and work on controls and demand-side flexibility. This section takes each in turn and ends with the gap they leave between them.

### 2.1 The peak, not the annual total, is the constraint

The GB literature agrees that electrifying domestic heat is limited less by annual energy than by the coincident peak on the coldest evenings. Early whole-system estimates were built from synthesised heat profiles. Sansom and Strbac put the 2010 building heat peak above 330 GW of heat and found that an electrified pathway would add 57 GW of electrical peak in a normal winter and about 74 GW in a cold one: "a near doubling of electricity peak demand" [@SansomStrbac2012].

Later work using measured profiles brought these numbers down without changing their direction. Watson, Lomas and Buswell, using half-hourly gas data from more than 6,000 homes, estimated peak GB domestic heat demand at 159–170 GW, about 40% below the earlier estimate of 277 GW, with a maximum ramp of 60 GW per hour, half the earlier figure, mainly because real cold-day profiles are flatter than the single peaky profile assumed before [@Watson2019]. A follow-up using about 600 heat pumps showed heat-pump operating patterns flatten demand further: in a cold year the heat peak falls to 157 GW and the ramp to 21 GW per hour [@Watson2021]. Converted to electricity, a fully heat-pumped stock would have a 75 GW heat-pump peak and take total GB demand from about 60 GW to 130 GW (less than the sum, because the two peaks do not fall in the same half-hour), or to 109 GW if hot water and a fifth of space heating were made flexible [@Watson2019thesis]. The main empirical diversity figure comes from Love et al.: across 696 heat pumps, after-diversity maximum demand was 1.7 kW per home, and 20% uptake would add 7.5 GW (14%) to the evening peak [@Love2017, pp. 332, 341].

Three points of agreement emerge. The heat peak is smaller than first feared; it is still large enough to roughly double system peak at full electrification; and its size depends heavily on how heating is operated. The disagreements are about method. The measured samples are not representative of the on-gas stock in which a hybrid would be installed: the Renewable Heat Premium Payment homes behind both Watson and Love were largely off-gas and social housing [@Love2017, pp. 332, 341]. And none of these studies runs more than a handful of weather years. Watson et al. use 2010 as their cold year; the Imperial work tests adequacy against a stylised one-in-twenty cold week [@Strbac2018]. The distribution of cold-spell peaks across many winters is asserted rather than measured.

### 2.2 What hybrids are claimed to do, and what they did

The modelling literature is close to unanimous that a boiler kept for the coldest hours is a cheap way to avoid peak capacity. For the CCC, Strbac et al. found the hybrid pathway the cheapest in 2050, at £81.6 billion a year against £87.8 billion for all-electric, while stressing that the pathways were within 10% of each other; the electric pathway needed about 100 GW more firm generation [@Strbac2018]. Vivid Economics and Imperial assumed hybrids "contribute little to the peakiness of electricity demand" because they "switch to gas mode during periods of high electricity demand" [@Vivid2019]. Element Energy, modelling for the then Department for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy, found hybrids "have the potential to significantly reduce the peak electricity demand compared to a standalone HP", although its comparison assumed the standalone heat pump fell back on resistive heating [@ElementEnergy2017].

The field evidence is thinner and more equivocal. **The Freedom project** in Bridgend remains the only British trial built around smart, price- and constraint-aware hybrid control. It added small (5–8 kW) heat pumps beside new combination boilers in 75 homes, with no changes to radiators or fabric [@FREEDOM2018]. It showed that fleet electricity demand could be reduced "to less than 20W per home within a few minutes … without impacting occupant comfort", and held a portfolio of homes to within 0.1–0.2 kW of a cap [@FREEDOM2018, pp. 15, 22]. Two of its findings bear directly on this paper. First, at 2017–18 prices cost-optimal control "strongly favour[ed] gas"; the heat pump's share reached about half only when gas prices were raised by 50% [@FREEDOM2018]. Second, the optimiser preferred continuous, gentle heat-pump operation with bursts of boiler, and the report described switching on outdoor temperature as "a relatively poor strategy" [@FREEDOM2018]. Its network modelling put upgrade costs at about £50 per customer per year for all heat pumps against under £5 for smart hybrids. These are modelled figures from a single winter, and its authors note that their pre-warming strategy was not tested head-to-head against temperature switching [@FREEDOM2018, p. 22].

**The Electrification of Heat demonstration** is the largest recent dataset. Its final median seasonal performance factor for air-source heat pumps, at the whole-system boundary (SPFH4, circulation pump and backup heater included), is 2.78 across 428 systems [@ESC2024, Table 1.2]; the interim figure was 2.80 [@ESC2023]. Heat pumps inside hybrids did worse: a median of 2.50 across 94 systems, supplying a median of only 40% of space heating, with performance falling as the heat pump's share fell [@ESC2024]. Although described as cost-optimal, the hybrids' takeover point "generally corresponded to a certain external temperature" [@ESC2024]. This sits within the 30–96% range of heat-pump shares that Element Energy compiled from earlier trials [@ElementEnergy2017]. Seven years before, the Carbon Trust's evidence review for government had already recommended "a floating (variable) bivalent point based on lowest cost, lowest emissions or lowest energy" [@CarbonTrust2016].

Measured flexibility from all-electric heat pumps is modest by comparison. In the EQUINOX trial, over 1,000 heat-pump homes on two-hour evening events delivered an average reduction of 0.6 kW per opted-in home, with under half opting in to each event [@NGED2024]. A hybrid can drop its heat-pump load to zero and keep the house warm on gas; an all-electric home cannot.

### 2.3 Carbon: average, marginal, and the missing controller

Policy accounting has moved from cautiously including the gas hybrid to excluding it. The Sixth Carbon Budget gave hybrids a real near-term role, conditional on "standards for smart controls" and on rebalancing gas and electricity prices [@CCC2020_6CB_Buildings, p. 63]. The Heat and Buildings Strategy said hybrids "could play a transitional role in the 2020s and 2030s", that it was "too early to rule hybrid systems in or out" for 2050, and that the evidence was too thin "to make firm conclusions about the potential carbon reductions that they can offer" [@HMG2021_HBS, p. 85]. It also noted that hybrid controls "can be used to optimise on the basis of cost or efficiency" [@HMG2021_HBS, p. 84], which is close to the controller studied here, but did not quantify it. The Seventh Carbon Budget and the 2025 Future Energy Scenarios then dropped gas hybrids from their central pathways [@CCC2025_7CB; @NESO2025_FES_Pathways].

How a hybrid's emissions are counted depends on which electricity emission factor is used, and the literature is clear that the choice matters. Government guidance reserves average factors for footprinting and the long-run marginal factor for "analysing sustained changes in energy consumption" [@DESNZ2026_Valuation_Background, para 2.9]. But that marginal factor assumes the change is "distributed evenly through the day and year" [@DESNZ2026_Valuation_Background, para 2.14], which a heating load is not, and the guidance asks analysts to treat peak effects only as non-monetised costs and benefits [@DESNZ2026_Valuation_Guidance, paras 3.18–3.19]. The academic work shows that short-run marginal factors in Britain differ substantially from averages: 0.69 kgCO₂/kWh for 2002–09 [@Hawkes2010], 0.26–0.53 for the following decade and diverging "very significantly" from the short run [@Hawkes2014], and from 29 to 469 gCO₂/kWh between regions in 2018 [@Pimm2019]. None of this work looks at the cold, still, high-demand half-hours in which a hybrid's choice is actually made, or at how often those hours occur across many winters.

The carbon value placed on the result also differs by an order of magnitude depending on the purpose. The government's central appraisal value for 2025 is £312 per tonne of CO₂-equivalent [@DESNZ2026_DataTables, Table 4]; the traded UK ETS allowance price is in the range £22–44 [@DESNZ2026_TradedCarbonValues]. A controller that weighs carbon must be told which price to use. This paper tests both.

Finally, the price ratio on which a hybrid's economics turn is now set as much by fiscal choices as by markets. In 2021 the government committed to "shift or rebalance energy levies … away from electricity to gas over this decade" [@HMG2021_HBS, p. 21]. What has happened instead is a move onto general taxation: from April 2026 the Exchequer meets 75% of domestic Renewables Obligation costs and the Energy Company Obligation is no longer levied on bills [@Ofgem2026_ECO_RO], and VAT on household electricity is suspended until March 2027 [@DESNZ2026_VAT]. The ratio of the electricity to the gas unit price under the cap peaked at 4.67 in early 2026 and fell to 3.30 by October [@Ofgem2025_Cap_JanMar26; @Ofgem2026_Cap_OctDec]. A fixed switching temperature cannot follow a ratio that moves by a third in a year.

### 2.4 Controls and flexibility in practice

The formal anchor for a hybrid's switching decision is capacity, not cost. European ecodesign rules define the bivalent temperature as the point below which the heat pump "requires supplementary capacity", and cap it at +2 °C for the average climate [@EU2013Reg813]. The MCS standard for hybrids requires only that the controls be "capable of prioritising the heat pump" [@MCS2024MIS3005D]. Neither requires the switch to respond to price, carbon or the grid. Manufacturers' controllers offer price-aware modes, but the defaults and fallbacks are fixed temperatures. Vaillant's hybrid manager ships set to an outdoor bivalence point of 0 °C for heating [@Vaillant2024Top10]. Daikin's integrated hybrid can choose by energy price, but with a third-party boiler it reverts to a fixed changeover temperature derived once from a static price ratio, with a worked example of 2.5 °C [@Daikin2026IRG, pp. 162–163], and its householders are asked to re-enter prices after any tariff change [@DaikinUK2021UG]. Mitsubishi's controller offers cost or carbon priority, with prices and emission factors typed in by hand [@MitsubishiND_FTC4].

The control literature shows that better is possible. Reviews of supervisory heat-pump control distinguish rule-based from model-predictive control, find cost reduction the most common objective of predictive control, and find a building's own thermal mass more cost-effective as storage than buffer tanks [@Pean2019]. In a randomised trial in British homes, automated heat-pump turn-down cut consumption in four-hour events by 32% with temperatures kept comfortable and householders "happy with the automation" [@Nesta2024HeatFlex].

Evidence from low-income homes points the same way. A 2026 study for the Committee on Fuel Poverty, combining interviews with 29 households, audits in 20 homes and monitoring data from over 1,100 installations [@Rivers2026CFP, p. 2], found that "where heating patterns are not well aligned to the tariff, time-of-use tariffs can increase fuel bills" [@Rivers2026CFP, p. 4]. Effective use of such tariffs "was confined to households with high technical engagement". The study's seventh recommendation is that time-of-use tariffs be recommended "only where the household has expressed clear confidence in managing the more complex arrangement or where automatic optimisation software or hardware is installed alongside", with the affordability case made at standard rates and time-of-use savings "treated as upside" [@Rivers2026CFP, p. 6]. Both conditions bear on this article: every result is reported on the flat standard variable rate as well as on the time-of-use tariffs, and the controllers tested are automatic optimisation software of the kind the recommendation describes.

Britain's main flexibility market, however, runs almost entirely by hand. The Demand Flexibility Service reached 2.6 million meters in 2023–24 and delivered 3.7 GWh, but over 99% of participants acted manually and the system operator attributes 91% of domestic delivery to reductions of 1 kW or less, "as opposed to any significant level of EV/heat pump participation" [@ESO2024DFS, pp. 4, 25]. Automated dispatch of heat, which Freedom showed to be straightforward for hybrids, has barely featured.

That is beginning to change through regulation. The Smart Secure Electricity Systems programme mandates smart functionality for electric heating, creates a licence for load controllers, and, for hybrids, attaches the obligations to the "common controller, or 'master controller'", leaving the boiler itself out of scope [@DESNZ2025SSES, p. 18]. Applications for the licence open in March 2027 and it becomes mandatory in March 2028 [@DESNZ2026Licensing, p. 9]. The programme specifies who may hold the controller and how securely, but not what the controller should optimise, and the consumer's right to override sits with the manufacturer's interface rather than in the licence [@DESNZ2026Licensing, p. 52]. Citizens Advice warns that "essential services such as heating must not become difficult to manage or understand" under automation [@CitizensAdvice2026SSES], which is, in substance, the comfort floor.

### 2.5 The gap

Taken together these literatures leave a specific and consequential gap:

1. **The hybrid in policy is assumed, not controlled.** The only quantified gas-hybrid case in UK carbon budgets fixes the heat pump's share at 80% or 50% [@CCC2020_6CB_Buildings]. Whole-system studies credit hybrids with near-ideal dispatch [@Strbac2018; @Vivid2019]. The largest field trial shows deployed hybrids running a fixed temperature rule and delivering 40% [@ESC2024]. The Freedom trial ran a cost-minimising controller in 75 homes for one winter on a two-rate tariff and estimated its value to the system [@Sun2019SmartHHP]; we found no study that derives the heat pump's share from a controller responding to half-hourly prices, weather and carbon across a national housing stock and a long weather record, and compares it with the fixed rules in use.
2. **Cold years are anecdotes.** Peak estimates rest on one to three weather years or a stylised cold week. The frequency with which a hybrid's boiler is decisive, for the bill or for the grid, has not been measured across a long weather record.
3. **The household, the climate and the grid are studied apart.** Bills are modelled at household level, carbon with flat annual factors, and peaks at system level with assumed dispatch. We found no study that reports all three for the same control rule across a national stock, so the trade-offs that Element Energy and Imperial warned of [@ElementEnergy2017; @Strbac2018] remain unquantified.
4. **Regulation is about to decide who holds the controller without evidence on what it should do.** The load-control licence arrives in 2027–28 [@DESNZ2026Licensing]; the value of the controller's objective is unknown.

This paper addresses all four, and its contribution is best stated against the three closest pieces of work. Element Energy's modelling for the government showed that hybrids can cut the peak a heat-pump stock adds, under assumed operation [@ElementEnergy2017]. The Freedom trial ran a cost-minimising hybrid controller in 75 real homes through one winter, measured how far their demand coincided, and held the aggregate under a cap; Imperial's modelling for it valued smart hybrid control to the whole system [@FREEDOM2018; @Sun2019SmartHHP]. Strbac and colleagues found the hybrid the cheapest route to decarbonised heat with near-ideal dispatch [@Strbac2018]. This paper does not add field evidence; Freedom remains the measured reference, and Section 4.10 tests the model against it. What it adds is the combination: a calibrated national housing stock, every winter since 1940, the controllers a hybrid could actually run, from the factory fixed switch to coordinated dispatch, and the household's running cost, the carbon and the national peak reported for the same rule.
