## 4. Method

The model is a half-hourly simulation of heat demand and heat supply for one monitored house and a sampled housing stock in __TOWNS__ towns across Great Britain, run through every heating year from __FIRST_YEAR__ to __LAST_YEAR__. It is written in Python with JAX [@JAX2018] and runs on a single NVIDIA DGX Spark, where one heating year for every town, home, tariff and controller takes about __SECONDS_PER_YEAR__ seconds. This section describes its inputs, its physics, the controllers it compares, how results are counted and scaled, and how it was validated.

{{fig:pipeline}}

### 4.1 Weather: every heating year since 1940

Outdoor temperature comes from the ERA5 reanalysis [@Hersbach2020], served hourly by the Open-Meteo historical weather API [@OpenMeteo_Historical; @Zippenfenig2023]. ERA5 begins in 1940 [@ECMWF_ERA5_1940], so the first complete heating year is 1 November 1940 to 31 October 1941. For each of the __TOWNS__ towns, from Penzance in the south-west to Wick in the far north, the model takes hourly 2-metre temperature for every heating year from 1940–41 to 2025–26, __N_YEARS__ years in all, and interpolates it to half-hours. Each year is run as a whole: mild autumn and spring days, summer days with only hot water to make, and cold snaps all count in proportion.

Each town also has a design temperature: the cold its heat pumps are sized for. MCS heat-loss calculations may use either a 99% or a 99.6% external design temperature from CIBSE Guide A; this model uses the colder, 99.6% basis [@MCS_DesignTemps, Table 2, column B] for the nearest of 14 listed locations, corrected by −0.6 °C per 100 m of altitude. Across the towns it ranges from __DESIGN_RANGE__.

### 4.2 Prices and carbon: one real year, matched by calendar day

Half-hourly tariffs and regional grid carbon do not exist for most of the weather record, so every weather year is priced on one real year: 1 November 2025 to 31 October 2026. Each simulated day takes the actual prices and carbon intensity of the *same calendar day* in that year, in the town's own electricity region: 1 December 1962 is priced as 1 December 2025. A leap-year 29 February borrows 28 February. The model therefore asks a precise question: *what would today's tariffs and today's grid have cost, and emitted, in the weather of each past year?* It does not reconstruct the prices of the past. Each simulated day therefore pairs a past winter's temperatures with a price, a wind output and a grid carbon intensity that were never observed together; the result is a conditional experiment, today's market in historic weather, and not a forecast of what the market of any past year would have done. The national demand to which the stock's heat is added (Section 4.6) is matched the same way, and so does not respond to the historic weather; Section 5.4 tests what that does to the peaks. Matching by date also puts a past Sunday on a 2025–26 weekday, whose prices and demand are higher. Pricing each day instead on the nearest 2025–26 day of the same weekday (Section 4.8) changes the share of homes for which the hybrid is cheapest by at most __REV_WDAY_HYB_MAXDIFF_PTS__ points on any tariff and its annual cost by at most £__REV_WDAY_GBP_MAXDIFF__, but raises the Agile hybrid's national peak in __REV_WDAY_AGILE_PEAK_UP__ of __REV_WDAY_YEARS__ winters, by a median of __REV_WDAY_AGILE_PEAK_MED_GW__ GW, and the herd above SD-Power's cap to up to __REV_WDAY_HERD_GW__ GW. Date matching therefore leaves the household results unchanged and, if anything, understates the herd.

Four electricity tariffs are compared:

- **SVR**, the price-cap standard variable rate: a flat __P_SVR__p/kWh all day;
- **Intelligent Octopus Go (IOG)**: __P_IOG_CHEAP__p/kWh from 23:30 to 05:30 and __P_IOG_PEAK__p/kWh otherwise, the Southern region's rates in force since 16 October 2026 [@OctopusIOG2026] (through 2025–26 the day rate was 28.9–30.4p, so IOG's dear daytime is a recent change);
- **Cosy** (Octopus's heat-pump tariff): the actual regional half-hourly rates, with cheap windows and a 16:00–19:00 peak [@OctopusCosy; @OctopusAPI];
- **Agile** (Octopus): a new price every half-hour that follows the wholesale market [@OctopusAgile; @OctopusAPI], __AGILE_HALFHOURS__ actual prices per region over the year, of which __AGILE_NEGATIVE_PCT__% were negative.

Gas is __P_GAS__p/kWh with a standing charge of __GAS_SC__p a day. The SVR and gas prices are the Ofgem cap for the Southern region, Direct Debit, October to December 2026, applied to every town; the GB average for the same period was 26.32p and 7.97p [@Ofgem2026_Cap_OctDec]. The gas standing charge is paid every day of the year by any home that keeps a boiler, and by no all-electric home. The electricity standing charge is paid by every home whatever its heating, so it is left out of every bill in the paper, as are the home's other electricity uses; bills are the cost of heating and hot water only. All prices are on one fiscal basis, today's, without VAT on electricity: the October–December 2026 cap for SVR and gas, the Intelligent Octopus Go rates in force from 16 October 2026, and the 2025–26 Agile and Cosy rates with the 5% VAT they carried until 30 September 2026 removed.

Grid carbon is the regional half-hourly intensity from the National Energy System Operator's Carbon Intensity API for the same calendar day [@CarbonIntensityAPI; @Bruce2024_CI_Regional]. Gas emits __GAS_KG__ kg CO₂ per kWh burned [@DESNZ2025_GHG_CF].

These 2025–26 prices are the basis of every headline result. One further, flat tariff is added for a single purpose, the fuel-poverty analysis of Section 5: each winter priced at *its own* prices. For every heating year with data, an all-in price per kWh of electricity and of gas (standing charges and VAT included), in 2025 money on the Consumer Prices Index, is taken from a series compiled for this paper from government price statistics, the Ofgem cap model, historical electricity data, parliamentary answers and the Bank of England's long-run dataset [@LDS2026_EnergyPrices]. Each all-in price is split into a unit rate and, for gas, a daily standing charge, using the unit-rate share of the year's government bill statistics (the 2010–15 mean before 2010). A heating year takes the mean of its two calendar years. This "historical" tariff answers a different question from the main runs: not what today's prices would cost in past weather, but what heating actually cost, at the time, as a share of income.

### 4.3 Heat demand

Each home's space-heating demand in each half-hour is its heat loss at the outdoor temperature less its internal gains:

<div class="eq">q_space(t) = max( UA × (T_set − T_out(t)) − G × g(t), 0 ) × 0.5 h</div>

where UA is the home's heat-loss coefficient (W/K), T_set its set point, G its internal gains from SAP 10.2 and g(t) a daily gains profile (0.7 overnight, 1.1 06:00–09:00, 0.8 during the day, 1.3 16:00–21:00, 1.0 late evening). The profile averages 0.94, so a home's mean gains are 6% below the SAP figure; the calibration to metered gas (Section 4.5) absorbs the difference in annual energy, but not in timing. Hot water is made in two two-hour reheats, 06:00–08:00 and 17:30–19:30. In the main runs each half-hour stands alone and heat is supplied when it is needed; a sensitivity run adds the building's thermal mass and lets the controller pre-heat (Section 4.8).

### 4.4 Heat pump efficiency and capacity

A heat pump's coefficient of performance (COP) is modelled as a share of the ideal Carnot COP at its flow temperature:

<div class="eq">COP(t) = share(T_out) × level × (T_flow + 273.15) / max(T_flow − T_out, 5), clipped to 1.3–7</div>

The Carnot share captures everything the ideal cycle leaves out, including defrost. For the housing stock it was fitted to __HPM_SYSTEMS__ British air-source systems on heatpumpmonitor.org [@HPM_org] that report space heating separately: __HPM_DAYS_ALL__ system-days over the winters of 2024–25 and 2025–26 passed the quality filters, every one of them inside the fitted temperature bands. Those systems hold a share of about 0.34–0.36 from −3 °C, the coldest band with enough days to fit, to 12 °C, falling to 0.31 when mild (Figure 2). Below −3 °C the model holds the coldest band's share; the capacity table, not the share, is what limits the heat pump there.

{{fig:share_fit}}

But the systems on heatpumpmonitor.org are, by self-selection, well installed and well tuned: their median seasonal performance factor is __HPM_FLEET_SPF__ across __HPM_FLEET_N__ British systems, against 2.78 in the national Electrification of Heat trial, measured over twelve-month windows between autumn 2020 and September 2023 [@ESC2024, Table 1.2 and p. 14]. The model's *level* scales the fitted Carnot share; what a home *realises* over a year also depends on its own flow temperatures, hot water at 48 °C and the weather. The two ends of the range are set accordingly.

- *The low end is calibrated on the realised figure.* Scaling the share by the ratio of the two measured performance factors (×0.72) gave the stock a median of __REV_CAL_SPF_OLD__ over the trial's three winters, below the trial's 2.78, because the stock's own flow temperatures lowered it a second time. The low level is therefore set at ×__LEVEL_LOW__, at which the stock's median home realises __REV_CAL_SPF_NEW__ over the same winters.
- *The high end is the fitted share itself* (×1.0): heat pumps as well tuned as the monitored fleet, but in the stock's homes, where they realise __REV_CAL_SPF_HI_2025__ in 2025–26 against the fleet's __HPM_FLEET_SPF__ in its own. The gap is consistent with the stock's higher design flow temperatures and larger share of hot water; it was not checked home by home.

Each sampled home draws its level uniformly between the two. That spread is an assumption, a range between two measured references, not a measured national distribution; the results at each end are reported alongside the central case (Sections 6.1 and 7.5). The trial's own half-hourly data, obtained after the calibration was set [@UKDS9209], give the measured spread: counted as all heat over all electricity, immersion and back-up heaters included, its __REV_EOH_SPF_Y1_N__ homes with a full year of readings realised a median of __REV_EOH_SPF_Y1_P50__ over the twelve months to September 2022 (10th to 90th percentile of homes __REV_EOH_SPF_Y1_P10__–__REV_EOH_SPF_Y1_P90__) and __REV_EOH_SPF_Y2_P50__ (__REV_EOH_SPF_Y2_P10__–__REV_EOH_SPF_Y2_P90__) over the year to September 2023. The medians confirm the published figure the low end was calibrated on, and the per-home figures computed here agree with the trial's own published ones for the same homes [@ESC2025_USmart] (median difference __REV_EOH_VS_ESC_MAD__ over __REV_EOH_VS_ESC_N__ homes, correlation __REV_EOH_VS_ESC_CORR__); the measured spread reaches further below the median than the model's, which is where poor installations sit. Counted as all heat delivered over all electricity used, hot water and any direct-electric top-up included, the median home realises a seasonal performance factor of __REAL_SPF_P50__ in the median winter (10th to 90th percentile of homes __REAL_SPF_P10__–__REAL_SPF_P90__), from __REAL_SPF_MAX_YEAR_VAL__ in the mildest winters to __REAL_SPF_MIN_YEAR_VAL__ in the hardest.

Flow temperature follows weather compensation, from 25 °C at 16 °C outside to the home's design flow temperature at its design outdoor temperature. Hot water is made at 48 °C. Output is capped by the unit's capacity, which falls in the cold, using a measured manufacturer table (Daikin ERGA06, 35 °C and 45 °C flow) scaled to each unit's size.

### 4.5 The housing stock

Each town samples __HOMES_PER_TOWN__ homes, drawn from that town's own housing rather than from assumed ranges.

**Heat loss from real dwellings.** In England and Wales the starting point is the Energy Performance Certificate heat demand of gas-boiler homes in every small area (2011 Lower Layer Super Output Area), by dwelling form (detached, semi-detached, terraced, flat) [@Canet2021_LSOA; @Canet2022]. Every small area whose population-weighted centre lies within 10 km of the town contributes, the radius widening in 2 km steps until at least 5,000 dwellings are included. Each area's demand is turned into a heat-loss coefficient using heating degree-hours from the same ERA5 record. Floor areas, and the spread of heat loss within each dwelling form, come from the government's anonymised National Energy Efficiency Data-Framework [@DESNZ2026_NEED_Anon]: for gas-heated homes of the same form and region, the mix of floor-area bands and each band's metered gas relative to the form's mean, so that size and heat loss vary together as they do in metered homes. Each town holds __STOCK_RECORDS_PER_TOWN__ such dwelling records, and each sampled home is one of them. The __STOCK_SCOT_PROXY_TOWNS__ Scottish towns, which the certificate dataset does not cover, take the North East of England's mix of homes scaled to Scottish metered gas, and are flagged as a proxy.

**Calibrated to metered gas, in the model's own terms.** Certificates over-predict what homes actually use [@Few2023], so each town's heat losses are scaled by a single factor. The factor is chosen so that *this model*, with its own heat-demand equation, internal gains, hot water and boiler efficiencies, run through that town's weather for the heating years __CAL_YEARS__, uses as much gas per gas-heated home as the government's metered figure for the same area [@DESNZ2025_MSOA_Gas]. That figure is weather-corrected to the gas industry's seasonal normal, which represented the expected climate of 2015–2020 [@DESNZ2026METH; @DECC2014WC; @NG2020GDFM], hence the calibration years. Gas meters outnumber gas-heated homes, so metered gas per meter is divided by __CAL_H_HEATED__ gas-heated homes per meter, the midpoint of the census range (Section 4.7). The factors range from __CAL_MULT_LO__ to __CAL_MULT_HI__ (median __CAL_MULT_MED__); the median home loses __STOCK_HTC_MEDIAN__ W/K.

**How people live, from surveys.** The other inputs are drawn from measured British evidence [@BRE2023SAP102]:

The indoor temperature is a 24-hour whole-house mean, not a thermostat setting: thermostats are set nearer 20 °C, but measured living rooms average about 19 °C and the rest of the house is cooler. Heat pumps are sized, as an installer would size them, to design room temperatures, not to the cooler temperature the home actually keeps. The daily gains profile is an assumption. For the pre-heating runs only (Section 4.8), each home's heat capacity is the SAP "medium" thermal mass, 250 kJ per m² of floor area per kelvin [@BRE2023SAP102], giving a median time constant of __STOCK_TAU_MEDIAN__ hours.

The same __HOMES_PER_TOWN__ homes are run through every day of every year, under every tariff and every controller. Each comparison is therefore *paired*: when the model reports that one controller beats another for some share of homes, it is the same homes, on the same days, in the same weather. A home's result is its whole-year bill, not any single day.

### 4.6 The controllers

Each half-hour, each controller decides which source supplies the heat. The all-heat-pump case is the benchmark: it uses only the heat pump, and any demand beyond its capacity is charged as direct electric heat at COP 1, so that the all-electric case is never flattered. Every hybrid is capacity-limited: when the heat pump is chosen but cannot meet the demand, the boiler tops up.

Here η is the boiler's efficiency, k_gas the gas emission factor, c(t) the half-hourly grid intensity and λ the carbon price. The C-COP rule is the decision a household optimising its own bill would make each half-hour with perfect knowledge of the current price and its heat pump's current efficiency: run the heat pump whenever its heat is cheaper than the boiler's. It is the controller studied in this paper's case-study house, where it is measured as the *commercial COP*. Because it chooses the cheaper source in every half-hour, with the same comfort, capacity and standing charge as the fixed switches, it cannot cost more than any of them over a year: that C-COP saves money is true by construction. What the model measures is how much it saves, for whom, in which winters, and what it does to carbon and the national peak. The savings are those of a perfectly informed controller with no switching losses, minimum run times or start-up penalties, and are an upper bound (Section 7.10).

**SD-Power dispatch** is the one controller that looks beyond the home. A price-aware controller in every home sees the same price at the same moment. On a flat tariff that is harmless, because C-COP hands the coldest hours to gas. On a dynamic tariff it is not: every home switches to its heat pump in the same cheap half-hour, and the stock's demand moves together, a problem regulation already anticipates with a randomised delay of up to ten minutes for smart heating [@DESNZ2025SSES, p. 7]. SD-Power dispatch is therefore defined against Britain's whole electricity system. For each half-hour of each weather year the model adds the stock's C-COP heat-pump demand, scaled to Britain's gas-heated homes, to the national demand the system actually carried in the same half-hour of 2025–26 [@NESO_HistoricDemand]. It sets a cap: the highest total that C-COP would produce in that year on a flat tariff. Wherever a tariff would push the total above the cap, a random share of homes, just large enough to hold the total at the cap, uses gas instead for that half-hour. This is the portfolio cap the Freedom project demonstrated on an average of 54 of its 75 homes [@FREEDOM2018, pp. 15, 22], applied nationally. It needs two passes of the model: the first measures the demand, the second dispatches against it. In every other respect the two passes are identical, and the second reproduces the first exactly for every other controller.

### 4.7 Counting, carbon and scaling

For each home, tariff and controller the model records the year's cost, gas and electricity use, the heat supplied by the boiler, and emissions on the average grid. Because electricity and gas use are recorded separately, emissions under any other constant factor can be recomputed without rerunning the model: the government's long-run marginal factor for 2025, 2030 and 2035 [@DESNZ2026_DataTables], the empirical short-run marginal range [@Hawkes2010; @Hawkes2014], and the 2030 and 2035 grids of the Future Energy Scenarios [@NESO2025_FES_Workbook]. These are accounting scenarios, not simulations of a future power system: the homes make the same decisions, against the same 2025–26 prices, and only the emission factor applied to their electricity changes. Results always name their basis: the *average* grid of 2025–26, half-hour by half-hour; a constant *long-run marginal* factor; or a constant *short-run marginal* factor.

Town results are reported as the share of the __HOMES_PER_TOWN__ homes for which each option is cheapest over the year, with the median home's annual cost. National electricity demand is the mean per-home demand in each half-hour, averaged over the towns of each of Britain's 14 electricity regions and weighted by that region's gas-heated homes (from __GB_GAS_METERS__ domestic gas meters in 2024, assigned to the regions small area by small area [@DESNZ2025_MSOA_Gas; @NESO2024_DNO_Areas; @ONS2021_MSOA_Centroids; @ScotGov2022_IZ_Centroids]). Gas meters are not quite gas-heated homes: in England and Wales the census counts 0.81 homes heated by mains gas alone per domestic meter, and 0.91 if homes that heat with gas alongside another fuel are included [@ONS2021_TS046]. The stock is calibrated per gas-heated home at the midpoint, __CAL_H_HEATED__ homes per meter (Section 4.5), so national demand scales by the same basis: each region's meters times __CAL_H_HEATED__, __GB_HEATED_HOMES__ gas-heated homes in all. (An earlier draft scaled per-home demand by meters, counting each home about 1/__CAL_H_HEATED__ times.) Peaks are the largest half-hour of each heating year under each controller, assuming every gas-heated home adopts it, and that no home's response moves the prices it faces (Section 7).

### 4.8 Sensitivity runs

Four further runs test the assumptions most likely to change the answer, and more were added in review. Except for the matched price years, each uses __SENS_YEARS__ weather years spread across the range of winter severity, from 1962–63 to 2025–26.

- **Matched price years.** Each heating year from 2016–17 to 2024–25 is run in its own weather *and* at its own prices instead of 2025–26's: that year's price cap (or, before the cap, Ofgem's illustrative rates) for SVR and gas, including the Energy Price Guarantee of 2022–23; Agile rebuilt from that year's half-hourly wholesale prices with today's Agile formula, the approach Octopus publishes and others have used [@Octopus2019_Agile; @Lyden2024]; Cosy and IOG as today's shapes scaled by the year's cap; and that year's regional carbon and national demand. This tests whether the conclusions depend on one price year [@Stute2024].
- **A smaller hybrid heat pump.** In the hybrids, the heat pump is sized at 0.55 of the all-electric unit. Installers price a hybrid as a boiler plus a smaller heat pump, because the boiler covers the coldest days [@DeltaEE2018]; 0.55 is this paper's choice, not a published ratio.
- **Moving the levies.** Flat electricity and gas prices of 34.3p and 8.17p, 20.4p and 8.17p, 16.3p and 8.17p, and 22p and 9.5p per kWh, from today's balance to the cases where policy costs move from electricity to gas or general taxation.
- **Thermal mass and pre-heating.** Each home becomes a single thermal node with its heat capacity (Section 4.5), and two controllers may warm it up to 3 K above its indoor temperature, never below, ahead of dear periods: all heat pump with pre-heating, and C-COP with pre-heating. A dynamic programme over each day's 48 half-hours chooses, for every home, how much heat to make in each half-hour and from which source, with the day's prices known in advance. Heat can be stored only from the heat pump, within its capacity; above capacity, the boiler or direct electric heat meets only the demand of the moment. The run uses 8,000 homes per town, a quarter of the main runs, because the programme is costlier.
- **Price feedback.** The headline runs take Agile prices as given. Here the stock's own demand raises them: each half-hour's Agile price gains __REV_FB_D__ × β × ΔD / 10 p/kWh (no VAT, on the paper's fiscal basis), where ΔD is the C-COP hybrids' national heat-pump demand on Agile in GW and β the within-year slope of wholesale price on national demand fitted over 2016–26 (winter, £__REV_FB_BETA__/MWh per GW); the stock is run again on the new prices, and the added price is updated with damping of 0.5, for six passes. The slope is fitted on the demand Britain has seen, so a second run doubles it (four weather years). Cosy and IOG are set by the supplier and are not fed back. 8,000 homes per town.
- **Heat-pump efficiency level.** Every home at the field-trial median level (×__LEVEL_LOW__); every home at the tuned fleet's (×1.0); and the main range with leakier homes given poorer installations, through a Gaussian copula with a correlation of −0.5 between the level and the logarithm of heat loss, so that the leakier a home, the lower its level tends to be. 8,000 homes per town.
- **Weekday-aligned prices.** Each weather day priced on the 2025–26 day of the same weekday nearest its calendar date (within three days), instead of the same date. 8,000 homes per town.
- **Staggered response.** The model's homes act on a price edge in the same half-hour (Section 4.10). Here each home's whole electricity schedule is shifted by its own random offset, uniform up to 30 minutes, one hour or two hours either way; with offsets independent of the homes, the expected national demand is the national series smoothed over that window, so the test runs on the main runs' national outputs for all __REV_STAG_YEARS__ winters. Britain's baseline is not shifted, and costs are not recomputed: it tests the peaks only.
- **Measured heating habits.** Each home keeps the habits of one home in the IDEAL study, drawn from __REV_IDEAL_LIB_DAYS__ measured winter home-days (Section 4.10): heating on whenever that home's was, a setback of 3 K otherwise, recovery limited by the heat pump's capacity through the home's own heat loss and thermal mass, and one hot-water reheat a day at the heat pump's capacity, overnight in half the homes and mid-morning in the rest (an assumption). The same __SENS_YEARS__ winters, 8,000 homes per town, with SD-Power dispatch replayed on the result as in Section 5.4.
- **Measured heat-pump habits.** The same test with the habits of heat-pump homes rather than boiler homes: each home keeps one home from the Electrification of Heat trial [@UKDS9209], drawn from __REV_H_LIB_DAYS__ measured winter home-days of __REV_H_LIB_HOMES__ non-hybrid homes (November to March, daily mean below 8 °C), heating on in every half-hour in which that home's heat pump delivered more than __REV_H_LIB_THR_W__ W of heat, with the trial's median evening-to-night setback of __REV_EOH_INT_SETBACK_P50_K__ K instead of 3 K. Hot water, recovery and the rest are as above. The library's homes heat for a median of __REV_H_LIB_HOURS_P50__ hours a day (__REV_H_LIB_HOURS_P10__ to __REV_H_LIB_HOURS_P90__ between the tenth and ninetieth percentiles), all day on __REV_H_LIB_ALLDAY_PCT__% of home-days and not at all overnight on __REV_H_LIB_OFFNIGHT_PCT__%. The same __SENS_YEARS__ winters, 8,000 homes per town, SD-Power replayed on the result.
- **Measured hybrid switching.** A further controller, the hybrid switched as the trial's __REV_EOH_HYBRIDS__ metered hybrids were (Section 4.10): each home a fixed changeover outdoor temperature below which the boiler carries the whole load, drawn so that the share of homes on gas at each temperature equals the boiler's measured share of the trial hybrids' heat in that 2 °C bin. The draw reproduces the measured curve to within __REV_H_BIVM_CURVE_MAXDIFF_PTS__ points; its median changeover is __REV_H_BIVM_CHANGEOVER_P50_C__ °C, __REV_H_BIVM_NEVER_BOILER_PCT__% of homes never use the boiler and __REV_H_BIVM_NEVER_HP_PCT__% never use the heat pump in the heating season, as the trial's shares at its coldest and mildest bins imply. Run with and without the measured heat-pump habits, the same __SENS_YEARS__ winters, 8,000 homes per town.
- **A local feeder.** __REV_LF_1962_FEEDERS__ low-voltage feeders of __REV_LF_HOMES_PER_FEEDER__ homes, made from the model's 4,000 sampled Birmingham homes shuffled at random, so that each feeder mixes leaky and tight homes; each home's load before heating is the measured winter-weekday electricity of one flat-tariff home in the Low Carbon London trial, a different measured day for each model day (__REV_LF_LCL_HOMES__ homes, __REV_LF_LCL_DAYS__ home-days [@UKPN2014LCL]; a diversified __REV_LF_LCL_ADMD_KW__ kW a home, __REV_LF_LCL_KWH_DAY__ kWh a day). Run on the town's __REV_LF_1962_DAYS__ coldest days of 1962–63 and of 2024–25, each home's electricity under every tariff and controller, with the national SD-Power dispatch shares of the main runs. A feeder's capacity is its own pre-heat-pump peak plus a headroom per home: none; 1.5 kW, the diversified design demand of a gas-heated home in the Debut method network designers use [@BEIS2022_LVCapacity, p. 52]; and 1.7 or 2.4 kW, the planning allowance per heat pump that NESO's consistent planning assumptions give distribution operators for a peak winter day [@NESO2026_CPA]. A local dispatch rule sends hybrids on an overloaded feeder to gas one home at a time, in random order, until the feeder fits, on its own and on top of the national dispatch. No voltage, phase or cable model: a count of feeders that would exceed a thermal limit.
- **A weather-matched market.** The main runs pair each historic day with 2025–26's prices, carbon and national demand on the same date (Section 4.2), so a cold still day in 1963 may meet a mild windy day's cheap power. Here each weather day instead takes the market of the day from 2016–17 to 2025–26 whose national mean temperature was nearest, among days of the same type (weekday or weekend) within 30 days of the same date in the year: its Agile prices (rebuilt from wholesale prices with today's formula before 2025), regional carbon intensity, national demand and wind output. Each is scaled by the ratio of its 2025–26 annual mean to that year's, so the run keeps today's levels and takes from the matched day only how the market moved with the weather. Cosy, a fixed-window tariff, is unchanged. Days colder than any since 2016 take the coldest available match: __REV4_WM_COLDER_1962__ days of 1962–63, which reached __REV4_WM_COLDEST_1962__ °C across Britain against __REV4_WM_POOL_COLDEST__ °C since 2016. Full stock, same homes.
- **SD-Power on residual demand.** The cap is set, and held, on national demand less transmission-connected wind (national demand counts only metered generation, so embedded wind and solar are already netted off [@NESO_HistoricDemand]) instead of on national demand itself, so homes are sent to gas only when the rest of the system is short, not when wind is plentiful [@NESO_GenMix]. Full stock, the __SENS_YEARS__ winters.

### 4.9 The monitored house

One real house anchors the calibration: my own, a three-storey house in Poole with a 6 kW Daikin Altherma monobloc on all-microbore pipework, a heat loss of 171.7 W/K measured from a year of its own data, and its own measured Carnot share (from __HOUSE_COP_DAYS__ days of daily COP), which falls from 0.35 in the cold to 0.21 in mild weather because the unit cannot turn down below about 1.5 kW and cycles. The model moves this house, unchanged, to each of the __TOWNS__ towns. It has no backup heater; any shortfall is charged at COP 1.

### 4.10 Validation

The model was checked against measured data at four levels before its version 3 national runs; version 4 changed none of the physics or inputs those checks cover. Two of the four are calibration checks rather than validation: the house's efficiency curve was fitted to the house's own data, and each town's stock is calibrated to its metered gas, so agreement there shows the fitting worked, not that the model predicts. The independent tests are the efficiency model against other people's heat pumps and the timing tests below.

**The house against itself (a calibration check).** Run through its own year of weather and its own heating schedule, the model predicts a space-heating seasonal COP of __HOUSE_MODEL_SCOP__ against __HOUSE_MEASURED_SCOP__ measured on heatpumpmonitor.org over the same period. An earlier version over-predicted mild-weather efficiency by about a fifth; the measured 12–16 °C point was added to the house curve to correct it.

**The efficiency model against real systems.** Using each system's own flow and outdoor temperatures, the stock's efficiency model was tested on __LITMUS_SYSTEMS__ heat pumps within 25 km of __LITMUS_TOWNS__ of the map towns, from the coldest to the mildest, over two winters. Below 6 °C it matched within 5%; in mild weather real systems did 10–20% better, which is why the stock uses the fleet's fitted curve rather than the case-study house's.

**The stock against metered gas (a calibration check).** By construction, each town's stock uses as much gas per gas-heated home as the government's metered figure over the calibration years (Section 4.5); the check that matters is that this holds with the model's own equations and inputs, not only with the certificate data, which an earlier version got wrong. Calibrating with certificate degree-hours at a 15.5 °C base while the model computed demand from each home's indoor temperature and gains left that version's stock using about 13% more gas than metered; the stock was recalibrated in the model's own terms before the final runs.

**Earlier versions.** The national model was first built for a public map [@LDSMap2026]. Its second version reproduced the first to the third decimal place on SVR, IOG and Cosy and corrected an Agile error: the first version compared heat-per-penny as a ratio, which on the __AGILE_NEGATIVE_PCT__% of half-hours with negative prices chose gas when electricity was being given away. The third version replaces the second version's assumed housing stock and inputs with the real, calibrated stock and sourced inputs of Section 4.5; its typical bills are about a quarter lower and its hybrid shares lower. The version reported here (v4) keeps that stock and physics and changes four things in response to review: one fiscal basis for every tariff (Section 4.2), national scaling by gas-heated homes rather than meters (Section 4.7), the efficiency range's low end recalibrated to the field trial's realised figure (Section 4.4), and the weather-matched and residual-cap tests of Section 4.8. Every number in this paper is from v4.

**An independent second implementation.** Because few readers can rerun a GPU model, the model's arithmetic was checked against a second implementation written separately, in plain Python, from the equations in this section, sharing no code with the model. For the monitored house, which involves no random sampling, the two agree in all __XCHK_HOUSE_PASS__ of __XCHK_HOUSE__ comparisons (__XCHK_TOWNS__; 2025–26 and 1962–63; every tariff and controller) to within £0.0001 a day. For the housing stock, __XCHK_HOMES__ homes drawn independently, from the same dwelling records and the input distributions of Section 4.5, reproduce the model's share of homes for which the hybrid is cheaper to within __XCHK_SHARE_PTS__ percentage points, and its median yearly costs to within __XCHK_MEDIAN_PCT__%, in all __XCHK_STOCK__ town–year–tariff comparisons. The check covers the historical-price tariff as well, runs on a laptop in a few minutes and is published with the model.

The controllers themselves are covered by known-answer tests: C-COP switches exactly at heat-per-penny parity, uses the heat pump at negative prices, the fixed bivalent rules ignore price, an extreme carbon price moves the choice to the lower-carbon source, grid-stress half-hours go to gas only under SD-Power dispatch, and only homes with a boiler pay the gas standing charge. The pre-heating controller is tested to never pre-heat on a flat price, to reduce to the plain controller without thermal mass, to never let a home fall below its set point, and to never make more heat in a half-hour than the heat pump's capacity or the heat needed to hold the temperature. That last test was added after the first pre-heating run, which let homes store heat from direct electric heating at up to about 39 kW on cheap half-hours; that run was discarded and repeated.

**What these checks do not establish.** The second implementation shows that the equations were coded as written, not that they describe real homes. The stock calibration fixes each town's mean annual gas use, by design, and says nothing about how heating demand is spread across the day or how far homes' demands coincide. Neither check validates the half-hourly shape of the national heat demand, its diversity between homes, occupant behaviour, or how real controllers respond at scale, and every national peak in this paper rests on those. Two published measurements from the Freedom trial in South Wales allow a partial test; the half-hourly data of the Electrification of Heat trial, obtained after the model was built, allow a direct one.

*After-diversity demand.* Freedom's final report, which combines the trial with network simulation, puts the potential peak load from conventional heat pumps at about 2–2.5 kW per house in a typical winter and 5 kW in extreme events [@FREEDOM2018, p. 69]. Across the __N_YEARS__ winters the model's all-electric stock adds __REV_ADMD_HP_MEDIAN_KW__ kW per gas-heated home to Britain's peak in the median winter, __REV_ADMD_HP_P95_KW__ kW at the 95th percentile and __REV_ADMD_HP_MAX_KW__ kW in the worst. The national all-electric peaks are therefore consistent with that estimate for a typical winter and, if anything, low for an extreme one. This is a plausibility check against a modelled range, not a comparison with measured demand.

*After-diversity demand, measured.* The Electrification of Heat trial's cleansed half-hourly data [@UKDS9209] cover __REV_EOH_HOMES__ homes from October 2020 to September 2023, with the whole heating system's electricity on one meter. On the __REV_EOH_COLD_DAYS__ coldest days with at least __REV_EOH_COLD_HOMES_MIN__ homes reporting (the median home's daily mean from __REV_EOH_COLDEST_C__ to __REV_EOH_COLD_TMEAN_MAX_C__ °C), the non-hybrid homes' coincident demand peaked at a median of __REV_EOH_ADMD_P50_KW__ kW per home across the days, and at __REV_EOH_ADMD_MAX_KW__ kW at __REV_EOH_COLDEST_PEAK_HH__ on __REV_EOH_COLDEST_DATE__, the coldest of them, against a median individual peak of __REV_EOH_IPEAK_P50_KW__ kW; the coincidence factor is __REV_EOH_CF_ALL__ across all homes, __REV_EOH_CF34__ for random groups of 34 and __REV_EOH_CF10__ for ten. The model's all-electric stock adds __REV_ADMD_HP_MEDIAN_KW__ kW per home in the median winter and __REV_ADMD_HP_P95_KW__ kW at the 95th percentile, so a measured __REV_EOH_ADMD_MAX_KW__ kW on a day near −5 °C sits where the model puts a cold winter. But the model's 34 all-electric homes on a flat tariff, even given measured heating habits (Section 4.8), have a coincidence factor of __REV_DIV_CF34_HP_SVR__ against __REV_EOH_CF34__ measured, so its heat pumps coincide more than real ones even without a price signal. The trial's pumps delivered __REV_EOH_HEAT_0006_PCT__% of their winter heat between midnight and 06:00, and the coldest day's demand peaked in the morning with a lower evening peak. These are trial homes with pumps sized to MCS rules, as the model's are; what the comparison tests is the model's diversity, not its stock.

*Aggregate demand, against the trial.* The direct test runs the model's all-electric homes on the trial's own days (Figure 3, Table 3). On the __REV_V_DAYS__ coldest days of winter 2022–23 with complete data for __REV_V_MEAS_HOMES__ non-hybrid trial homes (mean outdoor temperature __REV_V_MEAS_TEMP_C__ °C), 4,000 of the model's homes in each of __REV_V_TOWNS__, the map towns nearest the trial's three clusters, ran on the same dates, without and with the trial's heating habits (Section 4.8). Electricity a day agrees: __REV_V_MEAS_KWH_DAY__ kWh measured, __REV_V_BASE_KWH_DAY__ modelled. The coincident peak per home, which the national peaks are made of, is out by __REV_V_BASE_ADMD_ERR_PCT__% in the main runs (__REV_V_BASE_ADMD_KW__ against __REV_V_MEAS_ADMD_KW__ kW, the mean of the daily maxima) and by __REV_V_EOH_ADMD_ERR_PCT__% with the trial's habits (__REV_V_EOH_ADMD_KW__ kW). The agreement with habits is two errors cancelling. The model's individual peaks are out by __REV_V_BASE_IPEAK_ERR_PCT__% (__REV_V_BASE_IPEAK_KW__ against __REV_V_MEAS_IPEAK_KW__ kW), because a model home's demand is smooth within the half-hour, with no cycling, defrosting or hot-water bursts; and its homes coincide more: a coincidence factor of __REV_V_BASE_CF34__ for 34 homes in the main runs and __REV_V_EOH_CF34__ with habits, against __REV_V_MEAS_CF34__ measured, and __REV_V_EOH_CF300__ against __REV_V_MEAS_CF300__ for 300. The shape is the weakest part: the measured day peaks once, at __REV_V_MEAS_PEAK_HH__, and falls away through the evening; the main runs peak at __REV_V_BASE_PEAK_HH__ and again in the evening hot-water window, and their profile correlates with the measured one at only __REV_V_BASE_SHAPE_R__ (__REV_V_EOH_SHAPE_R__ with habits). For the headline peaks the test says this: on the main runs' assumptions the all-electric stock's addition to Britain's peak is overstated by about a fifth, and with measured habits it is about right, which is why those runs lower the worst-winter all-electric peak from __REV_H_BASE_PEAK_HP_SVR_MAX__ to __REV_H_EOH_PEAK_HP_SVR_MAX__ GW (Section 5.4); the national peak is the sum of per-home averages, so it does not inherit the coincidence error, but anything that depends on a home's own peak does. The herds on dynamic tariffs are a different quantity, made by a price edge rather than by cold, and the trial, on flat tariffs without automated switching, cannot test them.

*Coincidence.* Freedom measured a coincidence factor (the peak of a group's combined heat-pump demand over the sum of each home's own peak) of 0.27 for 34 hybrid homes on cold days under a cost-minimising controller on a two-rate tariff, falling towards 0.12 for very large groups, computed like the model's from half-hourly averages [@Sun2019SmartHHP, s. 4.1, 4.3]. The same calculation for 34 of the model's homes in __REV_CF_TOWNS__, the map towns nearest the trial, over the __REV_CF_COLD_DAYS__ coldest days of the trial's winter, gives __REV_CF34_CCOP_IOG_LO__ for C-COP hybrids on a two-rate tariff (IOG) and __REV_CF34_HP_LO__ for the all-electric home. The model's homes have no within-home variety: no thermostat schedules, cycling, differing occupancy or hot-water times. Each home's demand is smooth, and on a two-rate tariff every hybrid switches in the same half-hour. The national all-electric peak is a sum of average demands and agrees with Freedom's after-diversity estimate, but it is not free of this: with heating habits measured in real homes it falls from __REV_DIV_BASE_PEAK_HP_SVR_MAX__ to __REV_DIV_DIV_PEAK_HP_SVR_MAX__ GW over twelve winters (Section 5.4). And the real homes Freedom measured were far less synchronised than the model's. Every herd in this paper is therefore a synchronised stress case, not an estimate of what real homes would do. The coincidence factor cannot simply be applied to the national results, because it compares a group's peak with each home's own peak, which a smooth model home keeps low; instead the staggered-response run (Section 4.8) spreads the homes' timing directly (Section 5.4).

*Timing, against measured gas homes.* The IDEAL study recorded gas use at every meter pulse, with the boiler's pipe temperatures, in 255 homes around Edinburgh in 2016–18 [@Pullinger2021IDEAL; @IDEAL2021Data]. On the __REV_IDEAL_DAYS__ cold days of February and March 2018 shared by the most homes, chosen by Freedom's rule of at least one sub-zero reading (mean __REV_IDEAL_TMEAN_C__ °C), __REV_IDEAL_HOMES__ homes with complete data have a half-hourly coincidence factor of __REV_IDEAL_CF__ (__REV_IDEAL_CF10__ for ten homes), a median individual peak of __REV_IDEAL_PEAK_KW__ kW and __REV_IDEAL_KWH_DAY__ kWh of gas a day. The model's gas-boiler homes for Edinburgh on the same days use __REV_IDEAL_MODEL_BASE_KWH_DAY__ kWh a day, so the quantity is about right, but their coincidence factor is __REV_IDEAL_MODEL_BASE_CF__ and their individual peak __REV_IDEAL_MODEL_BASE_PEAK_KW__ kW: what the model lacks is timing. The measured-habits option (Section 4.8) supplies it from the measurements themselves. Each model home takes one IDEAL home's habits, heating whenever that home's central-heating flow pipe was above 35 °C on one of its measured winter days (__REV_IDEAL_LIB_DAYS__ days from __REV_IDEAL_LIB_HOMES__ homes, of which only __REV_IDEAL_OVERNIGHT_PCT__% mostly heat overnight). With a 14 kW boiler, chosen so that the model's individual peaks match the measured ones (so the peak match that follows is a calibration, not a test), the model's homes then have a coincidence factor of __REV_IDEAL_MODEL_LIB_CF__ (__REV_IDEAL_MODEL_LIB_CF10__ for ten), an individual peak of __REV_IDEAL_MODEL_LIB_PEAK_KW__ kW and __REV_IDEAL_MODEL_LIB_KWH_DAY__ kWh a day, and their average daily profile correlates with the measured one at __REV_IDEAL_MODEL_LIB_SHAPE_R__, against __REV_IDEAL_MODEL_BASE_SHAPE_R__ without habits.

The same habits do not bring the model's heat pumps near Freedom's figure: 34 all-electric homes on SVR have a coincidence factor of __REV_DIV_CF34_HP_SVR__, and 34 C-COP hybrids on IOG __REV_DIV_CF34_CCOP_IOG__, against 0.27. A heat pump sized to its home runs near its capacity for hours on a cold day, whatever its household's habits. Nor do the habits of heat-pump homes themselves bring it closer: with the trial's own on/off patterns and its __REV_EOH_INT_SETBACK_P50_K__ K setback (Section 4.8), 34 all-electric homes on SVR have a coincidence factor of __REV_H_CF34_HP_SVR__ and 34 C-COP hybrids on IOG __REV_H_CF34_CCOP_IOG__, against __REV_EOH_CF34__ measured in the homes the habits came from, so what separates the model from those homes lies within the half-hour and within the home, in cycling, defrosts and hot-water runs that a smooth model home does not have. The rest of the gap to Freedom's figure lies in how Freedom's controllers and tariffs behaved, with cheap windows that differ by meter, pre-heating ahead of each household's own schedule and frequent hand-over to gas, none of which the model reproduces. Habits measured in boiler homes may also over-diversify heat-pump homes, whose owners are commonly advised to heat continuously, and the surveys that measured each kind of home bear this out. In the 2011 Energy Follow-Up Survey the __REV_EFUS_JAN_HOMES__ logged homes with a heating pattern heated for a median of __REV_EFUS_HOURS_P50__ hours on a January weekday, __REV_EFUS_TWO_PERIODS_PCT__% in two periods and __REV_EFUS_OVERNIGHT_PCT__% overnight, and their living rooms fell by a median of __REV_EFUS_DROP_P50_K__ K between the end of the last heating period and the start of the first [@UKDS7471], close to the 3 K setback assumed here. In the heat-pump trial's __REV_EOH_INT_HOMES__ non-hybrid homes the evening-to-night fall of the one indoor sensor was a median of __REV_EOH_INT_SETBACK_P50_K__ K on winter days, and over 2 K in only __REV_EOH_INT_SETBACK_OVER2K_PCT__% of homes [@UKDS9209]. The measured-habit results (Section 5.4) are therefore a lower case for synchronisation, as the main runs are an upper one, and real heat-pump homes lie nearer the main runs than the habits of boiler homes suggest.

*Switching, against measured hybrids.* The trial also metered the boiler in __REV_EOH_HYBRIDS__ hybrid homes, whose controls were set to hand over to gas below a per-home outdoor temperature chosen as cost-optimal at pre-2022 prices [@ESC2024, pp. 11 and 73]. Over November to March the boiler supplied a median __REV_EOH_HYB_GAS_WINTER_P50__% of each home's heat (interquartile range __REV_EOH_HYB_GAS_WINTER_P25__–__REV_EOH_HYB_GAS_WINTER_P75__%): __REV_EOH_HYB_GAS_AT_M4C__% of the stock's heat at −4 °C, __REV_EOH_HYB_GAS_AT_0C__% at 0 °C, __REV_EOH_HYB_GAS_AT_4C__% at 4 °C and __REV_EOH_HYB_GAS_AT_8C__% at 8 °C, with both appliances running in __REV_EOH_HYB_BOTH_PCT__% of the half-hours in which either did [@UKDS9209]. At 2025–26 prices the model's C-COP hybrid gives the boiler __REV_MODEL_GAS_CCOP_SVR__% of the median home's heat on SVR, __REV_MODEL_GAS_CCOP_AGILE__% on Agile, __REV_MODEL_GAS_CCOP_COSY__% on Cosy and __REV_MODEL_GAS_CCOP_IOG__% on IOG, and a fixed switch at 0 °C gives it __REV_MODEL_GAS_BIV0_SVR__%. The installed hybrids of 2020–23 therefore behaved like the fixed switch this paper argues against, set near 8 °C by the price ratio of the time. The comparison does not show what the model's controller would do in those homes; it shows that a hybrid's gas share is a property of its control and its prices, not of the equipment. Section 5.4 runs the model's hybrids with the trial's switching and prices them at today's tariffs.

*Households acting on dynamic prices by hand.* The herd in this paper comes from automated controllers. The Low Carbon London trial shows what people do without them: in 2013 about 1,100 London households were sent High (67.20p), Low (3.99p) and Normal (11.76p) prices a day ahead by in-home display or text, while the rest stayed on a flat 14.228p [@UKPN2014LCL]. Comparing __REV_LCL_TOU_HOMES__ trial homes with __REV_LCL_STD_HOMES__ flat-rate homes, against the same two groups in 2012 before the trial, household electricity rose by __REV_LCL_LOW_W__ W a home (__REV_LCL_LOW_PCT__%) on average in __REV_LCL_LOW_EVENTS__ Low periods and fell by __REV_LCL_HIGH_W__ W (__REV_LCL_HIGH_PCT__%) in __REV_LCL_HIGH_EVENTS__ High ones. The response to a Low period built up over one to two hours, to at most __REV_LCL_LOW_PEAK_W__ W (__REV_LCL_LOW_PEAK_PCT__%), after a dip of up to __REV_LCL_LOW_PRE_DIP_W__ W while households waited for it. No more trial homes than flat-rate homes stepped up in the first Low half-hour (__REV_LCL_LOW_SHARE_TOU__% and __REV_LCL_LOW_SHARE_STD__%) or down in the first High one (__REV_LCL_HIGH_SHARE_TOU__% and __REV_LCL_HIGH_SHARE_STD__%), and the coincidence factor of 34 trial homes was __REV_LCL_CF34_LOW__ on days with a Low period and __REV_LCL_CF34_NORMAL__ on days without. People acting on a displayed price by hand shift a little demand, gradually and at different times; they do not herd. These are whole-household loads in 2013, and the dataset does not record how the homes were heated, so the trial does not measure what heat pumps would do; it shows the diversity that automated price response would remove.

### 4.11 Reproducibility

The model, its inputs, its tests, the independent second implementation and the scripts that turn its outputs into every number in this paper are published at https://github.com/CFLLDS/gb-hybrid-heat-model as release v1.0.0 (11 November 2026), with code under the MIT licence and text under CC BY 4.0; this version is built from model v4, and each run records the version that produced it. The release carries the model's per-winter outputs and its large inputs as downloadable assets, so every number can be regenerated without the GPU runs, and the run scripts for every stage so the outputs themselves can be reproduced. Of the four UK Data Service studies used, the Electrification of Heat monitoring data are open, and the heating-habits library drawn from them is included; the three safeguarded studies (EFUS 2011, Fuel Poverty 2023 and Households Below Average Income) cannot be redistributed, so only the aggregates the paper quotes are included, with the scripts that compute them from the studies for anyone registered with the service. Within that repository, every number in this paper is generated from the model outputs by a script and inserted at build time, so the text cannot drift from the results. Each year's run records its arguments, software versions and timing. The full run covered __HOUSEDAYS_TOTAL__ house-days and __DECISIONS_TOTAL__ half-hourly decisions per tariff for each of __N_CONTROLLERS__ controllers.
