## 5. Results

Every figure in this section is from the full run: __N_YEARS__ heating years, __TOWNS__ towns, __HOMES_PER_TOWN__ homes per town, priced at the 2025–26 tariffs unless stated. National figures weight each town by its region's gas-heated homes. "The hybrid wins" a home means its whole-year bill, gas standing charge included, is lower with a C-COP hybrid than with an all-electric heat pump. Here and throughout, "cheaper", "pays", "wins" and "saves" refer to the year's running cost, energy and the gas standing charge; equipment, installation, servicing, replacement and the cost of the gas network are outside the comparison (Section 7.9). The decision priced is therefore the one a household with a working boiler faces when a heat pump is installed: keep the boiler alongside it, or remove it. Buying a new hybrid system in place of a new heat pump is a different decision, with a boiler to buy, service and replace, and is not assessed. The hypotheses of Section 3 are tested in order, and each verdict is given as it stands, including where a hypothesis failed.

### 5.2 Software or hardware switching (RQ2)

C-COP is cheaper than every fixed changeover temperature on every tariff. Against today's typical factory setting, a fixed switch at 0 °C, it saves £__SAVE_BIV0_SVR_MEAN__ a year on SVR (__SAVE_BIV0_SVR_RANGE__ across winters), £__SAVE_BIV0_AGILE_MEAN__ on Agile, £__SAVE_BIV0_COSY_MEAN__ on Cosy and £__SAVE_BIV0_IOG_MEAN__ on IOG. The saving is largest on the tariffs whose prices move most within the day, which a fixed outdoor temperature cannot see. Not every hybrid is limited to the fixed switch: a Daikin integrated hybrid in its economical mode, with its stored prices kept up to date, makes the same price comparison as C-COP on a flat tariff, and can approach it on a tariff with up to three fixed price levels, such as IOG or Cosy (Section 1). Against such a controller the savings shown here apply only on Agile, whose price changes every half-hour, and whenever the stored prices have fallen out of date.

But C-COP emits more. Averaged over all winters, a C-COP hybrid on SVR emits __MEAN_KG_CCOP_SVR__ kg of CO₂ a year against __MEAN_KG_BIV0_SVR__ kg for the 0 °C fixed switch, and on Agile __MEAN_KG_CCOP_AGILE__ kg against __MEAN_KG_BIV0_AGILE__ kg. At today's prices gas heat is often cheaper per kWh than heat-pump heat even in mild weather, so a controller that follows price burns more gas than one that follows temperature.

**H2's cost half could not fail, and its carbon half fails.** C-COP is given the same information as a fixed switch and picks the cheaper fuel in each half-hour, so it cannot cost more than any fixed rule; that it is cheaper is true by construction, not a finding. On a flat tariff it is also less than software: with one electricity and one gas price, C-COP runs the heat pump whenever its efficiency exceeds the price ratio times the boiler's efficiency, __COP_BREAKEVEN_SVR__ for a typical boiler, and under weather compensation that is a changeover temperature fixed for each home. The £__SAVE_BIV0_SVR_MEAN__ a year on SVR is therefore the value of setting that changeover point correctly at commissioning; the best of the three fixed settings tested, __BEST_BIV_SVR_C__ °C, already recovers all but £__SAVE_BESTBIV_SVR_MEAN__ of it. Software earns its keep only where prices move within the day: £__SAVE_BIV0_AGILE_MEAN__ on Agile, where no fixed setting can follow the price. The test that could fail is carbon, and there H2 fails: on every tariff C-COP emits more than the 0 °C switch. ### 5.3 Carbon (RQ3)

Keeping the boiler costs carbon. On SVR and today's average grid, a C-COP hybrid emits __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_NOW_SVR__ kg a year more than an all-electric heat pump; on Agile, __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_NOW_AGILE__ kg more. As the grid decarbonises the gap widens. Measured on one basis, the government's long-run marginal factors, it is __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_2025_SVR__ kg in 2025, __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_2030_SVR__ kg in 2030 and __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_2035_SVR__ kg in 2035 on SVR (__KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_2030_AGILE__ and __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_2035_AGILE__ kg on Agile). Only if each kWh of heat-pump electricity is charged at a gas-plant marginal factor of 0.53 kg does the hybrid come out ahead, emitting __KG_HYB_AHEAD_MARG053_SVR__ kg a year less on SVR, and that factor describes a grid Britain is leaving. The 2030 and 2035 figures substitute those years' emission factors into today's consumption; they are not a simulation of the future system, whose prices, dispatch and peaks would also change.

Pricing carbon closes most of the gap. At the government's appraisal value of £312 a tonne, carbon-aware C-COP closes __C312_GAP_CLOSED_PCT_SVR__% of the gap on SVR (__MEAN_KG_CCOP_SVR__ kg down to __MEAN_KG_CCOP_C31_2_SVR__ kg) and __C312_GAP_CLOSED_PCT_IOG__–__C312_GAP_CLOSED_PCT_COSY__% on the time-of-use tariffs. It costs the household £__C312_PREMIUM_SVR__ a year more than plain C-COP on SVR, £__C312_PREMIUM_AGILE__ on Agile, £__C312_PREMIUM_COSY__ on Cosy and £__C312_PREMIUM_IOG__ on IOG.

**H3 is supported**, with one qualification. The hybrid emits more than the heat pump on today's average grid and the gap widens as the grid decarbonises, and a carbon price at the appraisal value closes most of it. Whether £__C312_PREMIUM_SVR__ to £__C312_PREMIUM_IOG__ a year is the "modest cost" the hypothesis predicted is a judgement the reader can make; for a household in fuel poverty it is not small.

### 5.4 Grid flexibility (RQ4)

If every gas-heated home in Britain heated with an all-electric heat pump, the largest half-hour of national demand would average __PEAK_MEAN_HP_SVR__ GW across the winters, reaching __PEAK_HP_SVR_MAX__ GW in the hardest, against __PEAK_MEAN_BASE__ GW for the system as it is. The tariff makes no difference to this, because the all-electric home heats when it must.

A C-COP hybrid stock on SVR brings the average peak down to __PEAK_MEAN_CCOP_SVR__ GW, removing __PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_SVR__% of the heat-driven increase: on a flat tariff, C-COP gives the coldest hours, which are also the peak hours, to gas. On dynamic tariffs it does not. Every home sees the same cheap half-hour at the same moment and turns its heat pump on together; the stock's demand piles into the cheap periods, overnight and at dawn, and the national peak rises to __PEAK_MEAN_CCOP_AGILE__ GW on Agile, __PEAK_MEAN_CCOP_COSY__ GW on Cosy and __PEAK_MEAN_CCOP_IOG__ GW on IOG on average, and up to __PEAK_CCOP_IOG_MAX__ GW. Price-aware hybrids on dynamic tariffs remove only __PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_COSY__–__PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_IOG__% of the heat-driven peak. This is the herding that simulation studies have long predicted for price-responsive heat pumps and that time-of-use trials have measured [@Kelly2014; @Georges2016; @ElementEnergy2014_HEUS; @Muratori2016], and the over-synchronised demand that recent policy analysis warns of [@Schittekatte2026].

SD-Power dispatch removes it. Holding Britain's total to the flat-tariff hybrid's peak, it keeps the average peak at __PEAK_MEAN_SDP_AGILE__ GW on every tariff and the highest at __PEAK_SDP_AGILE_MAX__ GW in the hardest winter, against __PEAK_CCOP_AGILE_MAX__ GW for uncoordinated C-COP on Agile. ### 5.5 Fuel poverty: heating at the prices of the time

The historical-price tariff (Section 4.2) puts each winter's heating bill, for the median home, against the income of the time. The income is the upper boundary of the poorest tenth, the 10th percentile of equivalised disposable household income (__BURDEN_P10_INCOME_2025__ in 2025–26, in 2025 money), from the ONS series since 1977 and the Family Expenditure Survey microdata before it (Section 7.8), so these burdens are those of the best-off households in the poorest tenth; households further down it carry more. The bill is at each winter's own prices from the historical series, not the main runs' October 2026 cap: in 2025–26 it is £__BURDEN_GAS_MEDIAN_2025__ a year for the median home heating with gas alone. On that basis heating with gas alone took __BURDEN_P10_GAS_1962_PCT__% of income in 1962–63, when the coldest winter in the record met the lowest incomes, __BURDEN_P10_GAS_1977_PCT__% in 1977–78, __BURDEN_P10_GAS_2010_PCT__% in 2010–11, __BURDEN_P10_GAS_2022_PCT__% in 2022–23, the highest of the winters since 1977, and __BURDEN_P10_GAS_2025_PCT__% in 2025–26. Across the __BURDEN_P10_YEARS__ winters with income data, from __BURDEN_P10_FIRST_YEAR__, it ranged from __BURDEN_P10_GAS_MIN_PCT__% to __BURDEN_P10_GAS_MAX_PCT__%.

The all-electric heat pump would have been a worse bet in the 1970s and 1980s, when electricity was dear relative to gas: its heating burden at the 10th percentile peaks at __BURDEN_P10_HP_MAX_PCT__% in __BURDEN_P10_HP_MAX_YEAR__. The C-COP hybrid, able to use whichever fuel was cheaper in each half-hour, can never cost more than gas alone, so the comparison that can fail is with the heat pump: the hybrid's bill is the lower in __BURDEN_P10_CCOP_LOWEST_YEARS__ of the __BURDEN_P10_YEARS__ winters, and its highest burden, __BURDEN_P10_CCOP_MAX_PCT__% in __BURDEN_P10_CCOP_MAX_YEAR__, is the lowest peak of the three. For a household that cannot absorb a bad year, the hybrid is a hedge against the price ratio as well as against the weather.

The ranking of the three options is for a typical home at the prices of each winter and is the same at every income; income enters only as the denominator of the burden. These are heating burdens, not the official measure of fuel poverty, which counts all household energy and, in England, is defined by low income and low energy efficiency together [@Hills2012; @DESNZ2026_FuelPoverty]. They use a national median home's heating, not the larger homes and lower efficiencies of many fuel-poor households, and the 2022–23 figures exclude the £400 Energy Bills Support Scheme credit, which was paid as a bill credit rather than a lower price, so they overstate that winter's burden. The finding they support is a relative one: across more than sixty years of real prices, a household that kept the choice of fuel was rarely the one paying most.
