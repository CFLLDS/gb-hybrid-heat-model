## 6. Discussion

The results answer the four research questions, but the questions were asked for a reason: to inform decisions about heating, flexibility and regulation that Britain is making now. This section draws out what the results mean for those decisions, and where they cut against both the case for hybrids and the case against them.

### 6.1 The hybrid question is a price question, and a commissioning question

The single strongest result is that the tariff, not the town and not even the winter, decides whether keeping a boiler pays (Section 5.1). Behind the tariff sits one number: the ratio of the electricity price to the gas price. A heat pump with a seasonal efficiency of 3 beats a 92% boiler only while electricity costs less than about 3.3 times as much as gas per kWh [@Pollitt2026]. The same ratio sets the point at which a C-COP controller hands heat to the boiler. Through most of the past decade Britain's ratio sat well above it; at the start of 2026 it was 4.7 [@Pollitt2026]. The Ofgem cap for October to December 2026, after VAT was removed from household electricity and three-quarters of the Renewables Obligation was moved from bills to the Exchequer, puts it at 3.30 across Britain [@Ofgem2026_Cap_OctDec; @Ofgem2026_ECO_RO], and __PRICE_RATIO_SVR__ in the Southern region whose prices the model uses. Pollitt's break-even assumes a seasonal efficiency of 3 and a 92% boiler. In the model's own terms, with the stock's realised median seasonal performance of __REAL_SPF_P50__ (Section 4.4) and a typical 84% boiler, break-even falls at a ratio of about __BREAKEVEN_RATIO_MODEL__, so today's prices sit on the heat pump's side of it for the typical home and on the boiler's side for the least efficient installations. That is why, over the same nine winters, the hybrid wins __SENS_MATCHED_HYB_SVR_MAIN_RANGE__% of homes on SVR at today's prices against __SENS_MATCHED_HYB_SVR_RANGE__% at the prices of each winter from 2016 to 2025, and why the levy sweep flips the answer completely within a few pence.

Two conclusions follow, pulling in different directions. For the household, the hybrid's value has fallen sharply with the 2026 levy changes, and further rebalancing of policy costs, which several analyses recommend [@Nesta2024_Levies; @Pollitt2026], would remove it on a flat tariff altogether. For the system, the hybrid's value is the opposite of a bet on today's ratio: it is insurance against that ratio moving. A household with both a heat pump and a boiler, and a controller to choose between them, does not need to know what the ratio will be. Across the __BURDEN_P10_YEARS__ winters for which incomes can be compared, at the prices of each winter, the C-COP hybrid's heating bill was below the all-electric heat pump's in __BURDEN_P10_CCOP_LOWEST_YEARS__ (Section 5.5); by construction it is never above gas alone. Insurance has a price, and it should be stated plainly: the gas standing charge, about £__GAS_SC_GBP_YEAR__ a year at today's cap, which a hybrid recovers only where its energy saving over an all-electric heat pump exceeds it. That one comparison explains most of RQ1, and in a year like 2025–26 the policy is not claimed on.

The second strongest result is the heat pump's own efficiency (Section 7.5). With every home at the national field-trial median, the hybrid is the cheaper to run for __REV_COP_LO_HYB_SVR__% of homes on SVR; with every home as well tuned as the heatpumpmonitor.org fleet, for __REV_COP_HI_HYB_SVR__%; the central case, drawing each home between the two, gives __REV_COP_MID_HYB_SVR__% over the same twelve winters (__HYB_WINS_SVR_MEDIAN__% in the median of all __N_YEARS__). That range is far wider than the effect of the town or the winter, and the central figure should not be read without it. Which end describes Britain depends on the population in question. For heat pumps installed in recent years in ordinary homes, the field-trial median is the better-evidenced level: the Electrification of Heat trial sampled a range of British housing [@ESC2024], while heatpumpmonitor.org systems are self-selected by owners who monitor and tune them. For a future stock the level is a policy choice, set by installation and commissioning standards. On the evidence here, even a stock at the field-trial median leaves the all-electric heat pump the cheaper to run for most homes on a flat tariff; the household case for keeping a boiler on a flat or wholesale tariff is a hedge against an installation poorer than that median, and well-commissioned heat pumps remove it. On IOG the hybrid keeps an advantage for most homes either way, but part of that advantage is the all-electric home's inability to avoid IOG's daytime rate without storing heat: when both homes may pre-heat, the hybrid wins __REV_PRE_HYB_IOG__% (Section 7.3). Better commissioning would therefore do at least as much for household running costs as any tariff, and it pulls in the opposite direction from a general case for hybrids.

Running cost is not whole-life cost. Keeping a boiler means servicing it, about £70 to £150 a year [@FantasticServices2026_Service; @FuseEnergy2026_Service], and replacing it, £1,800 to £3,600 installed for a like-for-like combi [@FantasticServices2026_Replace], every 12 to 15 years: £__REV_WL_KEEP_LO__ to £__REV_WL_KEEP_HI__ a year, say £__REV_WL_KEEP_MID__, on top of the gas standing charge the model already counts. Against that, the hybrid's running-cost advantage, where it has one, is small. In the median winter the tenth of homes that gain most from keeping the boiler __REV_WL_P90_SVR__ a year on SVR, __REV_WL_P90_AGILE__ on Agile, __REV_WL_P90_COSY__ on Cosy and __REV_WL_P90_IOG__ on IOG; the median home's gain is £__REV_WL_GAP_P50_IOG__ on IOG and it loses on the other three. On the flat tariff and on Agile the servicing bill alone wipes out the running-cost case for nearly every home; only on IOG does it survive, and there only for a boiler that is already installed and paid for. A home choosing between a new hybrid and a new heat pump faces the boiler's purchase as well, which this paper does not price (Section 7.9); on running costs plus upkeep, the bounded answer is that the hybrid pays only on a tariff with dear daytime electricity.

### 6.2 Software beats hardware, but software for what?

Every hybrid sold today can be switched by software, and the results show it should be. Some already compare stored prices, and on a flat or fixed time-of-use tariff such a controller, kept up to date, captures most of what C-COP does; the factory default of others is a fixed temperature. Against that default C-COP is cheaper on every tariff, by £__SAVE_BIV0_SVR_MEAN__ to £__SAVE_BIV0_IOG_MEAN__ a year against the common 0 °C default (Section 5.2). The Freedom trial showed the same in practice: a cost-optimising controller pre-heated with the heat pump on cheap night rates and handed the dear daytime hours to the boiler [@Sun2019SmartHHP; @FREEDOM2018]. The Electrification of Heat trial's hybrids show the other side: switched at fixed per-home temperatures set for pre-2022 prices, they gave the boiler a median __REV_EOH_HYB_GAS_WINTER_P50__% of their winter heat [@UKDS9209], and a stock switched that way at today's prices would pay £__REV_H_BIVM_CCOP_SAVE_BIVM_SVR__ to £__REV_H_BIVM_CCOP_SAVE_BIVM_AGILE__ a year more than under C-COP and emit __REV_H_BIVM_KG_OVER_CCOP_SVR__ kg more CO₂ (Section 5.4). A changeover temperature is a price ratio frozen at commissioning; when the ratio moves, the setting is wrong until someone visits.

But a controller that minimises the household's bill burns more gas than one that follows the outdoor temperature, because at today's prices gas heat is often the cheaper kWh. The objective the controller is given decides whether software helps the climate. A carbon-aware objective, pricing CO₂ at the government's appraisal value, removes most of the hybrid's carbon penalty (Section 5.3), at a cost to the household of £__C312_PREMIUM_AGILE__ to £__C312_PREMIUM_IOG__ a year depending on tariff.

This is a regulatory gap, not a technical one. The Smart Secure Electricity Systems programme will license load controllers from 2028 and specify who may hold them, how securely and how the consumer overrides them [@DESNZ2026Licensing; @DESNZ2025SSES]. It does not specify what they should optimise. For hybrids, that choice is worth hundreds of kilograms of CO₂ per home per year, and the market will not make it unaided, because the household pays for carbon only through the price it is charged. Germany's rules show the alternative: a hybrid counts towards its renewable-heat requirement only if it runs with priority for the heat pump, under a shared, remotely addressable controller, with a heat pump sized for at least 30% of the heat load (40% if the two never run together); a heat pump added to a working condensing boiler is exempt from these conditions [@dena2026_hybrid, p. 3]. The Netherlands, which had planned to require hybrids from 2026, dropped the requirement [@Rijksoverheid2024_hybrid], even though its own demonstration homes had cut their gas use by three-quarters [@SSEF2024_hybrid_demo]. In neither country, and not yet in Britain, is the controller's objective set by rule.

### 6.3 The herd

The most consequential result for the grid was not anticipated when the research questions were written (Section 3). Price-aware controllers in every home, all seeing the same price, synchronise. On dynamic tariffs a C-COP hybrid stock removes only __PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_COSY__–__PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_IOG__% of the heat-driven peak, against __PEAK_REMOVED_CCOP_PCT_SVR__% on a flat tariff, and the uncoordinated peak reaches __PEAK_CCOP_IOG_MAX__ GW in the worst winter (Section 5.4). Letting every home pre-heat makes it worse. These are stress cases, in which the modelled homes act together far more than real ones do; spreading their timing by up to two hours shrinks the herds on Agile and Cosy, but not on IOG, and measured household habits shrink them by about a third, and IOG's by a tenth. This is not new as a mechanism: simulation studies found it a decade ago in British and Belgian housing [@Kelly2014; @Georges2016], a British time-of-use trial measured a new peak at the start of the cheap band [@ElementEnergy2014_HEUS], while London households who were sent dynamic prices but had to act on them by hand shifted a few per cent of their demand, gradually and without synchronising (Section 4.10), studies of automated home energy management predicted "pronounced rebound peaks" [@Muratori2016], and recent work on distribution networks finds that time-varying tariffs cut reinforcement costs only up to an adoption threshold, beyond which they raise them [@Zadoks2026; @Schittekatte2026]. The herd is a product of automation: the controllers that make price response reliable also remove the diversity that hand-operated response keeps. What this paper adds is its scale for Britain's heating, across the full weather record, and the cost of preventing it.

Its fuel cost is small. Idealised SD-Power dispatch, sending just enough homes to gas in just enough half-hours to hold Britain's total at the flat-tariff peak, removes __HERD_EXCESS_AGILE_GW__ GW from the worst-winter peak on Agile and __HERD_EXCESS_IOG_GW__ GW on IOG, for about £__SDP_COST_AGILE_MEAN__ a home a year in extra fuel and a few kilograms of CO₂; over twelve test winters, a cap on demand net of wind costs about £__REV4_RESID_COST_AGILE__ against £__REV4_GROSS_COST_AGILE__, because it sends homes to gas less often and seldom when the wind is blowing, but it lets the gross peak, which the networks carry, rise from __REV4_GROSS_GROSS_PEAK_AGILE__ to __REV4_RESID_GROSS_PEAK_AGILE__ GW. Three qualifications from Section 5.4 bound that result. The full herd needs every home on the same dynamic tariff: spread evenly across four tariffs, the uncoordinated peak exceeds the cap by at most __REV_MIX_EVEN4_OVER_GW__ GW. Coordination has to be scheduled, not reactive: an operator that sets each half-hour's shedding from the one before keeps only __REV_SDP_AGILE_LAG1_GW__ GW of the __REV_SDP_AGILE_IDEAL_GW__. And the fuel cost is not the cost of delivery. What delivery would cost is not known; Table 12 shows what it would have to be. A randomised start delay of up to ten minutes, the protection the smart-appliance rules provide [@DESNZ2025SSES, p. 7], cannot break up a herd that lasts for hours. The design question is governance: who sets the cap, and who may call homes to gas. The answer this paper's evidence supports is the one Apostolopoulou describes as hybrid governance: an authoritative focal point manages the systemic risk that individual price signals cannot see, and prices coordinate everything else [@Apostolopoulou2026]. SD-Power is one implementation of that idea for heat. Electricity demand is not very responsive to price on its own [@WaddamsPrice2026]; automated control is what makes heat flexible, and automated control is also what makes it herd.

### 6.4 Flexibility markets have not yet priced heat

Britain's flexibility ambitions depend on exactly this kind of demand. The Clean Power 2030 plan expects consumer-led flexibility to grow from 2.5 GW in 2023 to 10–12 GW by 2030 [@DESNZ2024CP2030]. A hybrid stock under coordinated control is, in effect, a very large flexible load: in any half-hour, its heat-pump demand can be moved to gas. The __HERD_EXCESS_AGILE_GW__ GW of Section 5.4 is not a measure of that flexibility. It is the herd that synchronised tariffs create and that coordination removes, and setting it against the Clean Power 2030 target would count the cure of a self-made problem as a resource. Even a small fraction of Britain's gas-heated homes on coordinated hybrids would nonetheless be a material contribution to that target, and unlike a battery it comes with its own fuel store.

The markets that pay for flexibility today do not reach it. The Demand Flexibility Service was more than 99% manually initiated in 2023–24, with almost no heat-pump participation [@ESO2024DFS]. The most generous household scheme, paying £1 per kWh, pays for battery export during grid events, not for heat moved from electricity to gas [@Axle2026VPP]. A hybrid that switches to its boiler for an hour in a stress event delivers exactly the reduction those schemes seek, and is paid nothing for it. Pricing that service, whether through the Demand Flexibility Service, local network flexibility markets or the load-control licence, is a precondition for SD-Power at scale, and would also offset the gas standing charge that today makes the hybrid a poor bet for many households.

### 6.5 Carbon: a bridge with an expiry date

Keeping the boiler costs carbon, and the cost rises as the grid decarbonises: from __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_NOW_SVR__ kg a year per home today to __KG_GAP_CCOP_HP_2035_SVR__ kg on the 2035 grid on SVR (Section 5.3). The hybrid is therefore a bridge, not a destination, and its case weakens every year the grid gets cleaner and the climate warmer (Section 5.1). Two things could lengthen the bridge: a carbon-aware controller, which closes most of the gap, and SD-Power dispatch, which costs little carbon because it sends only a small share of homes to gas in each stressed half-hour (Section 5.4). Neither makes a gas hybrid net zero. What they can do is let Britain avoid building generation and network capacity for a peak that gas can serve, the reason whole-system studies found hybrid pathways cheapest [@Strbac2018], while the stock of all-electric homes grows. Whether that is worth the cost of keeping the gas network open for hybrid homes, and who pays as its fixed costs fall on fewer customers [@Ofgem2023_SSMC_Finance; @DavisHausman2021], is outside this model and is the most important unanswered question in the hybrid debate.

### 6.6 Fuel poverty: automation, not tariffs, protects the vulnerable

The Committee on Fuel Poverty's recent review found that time-of-use tariffs can raise bills for households whose heating is not aligned to the cheap periods, and recommended them only where households are confident managing them "or where automatic optimisation software or hardware is installed alongside", with the affordability case made at standard rates [@Rivers2026CFP, pp. 4, 6]. The results here support both halves of that recommendation. At standard rates, the hybrid's advantage is modest and depends on the winter; the larger savings on time-of-use tariffs (Sections 5.1 and 5.4) come from automation that no household should be asked to perform by hand. A carbon-aware controller, however, adds £__C312_PREMIUM_SVR__ a year on SVR, which for a household in fuel poverty is not small. If carbon is to be priced into the controller's choice, the cost should not fall on those least able to bear it.

The historical burden analysis adds a longer view. A household at the upper boundary of the poorest tenth would have spent from __BURDEN_P10_GAS_MIN_PCT__% to __BURDEN_P10_GAS_MAX_PCT__% of its income on heating with gas since 1961. The worst winter for all three options is __BURDEN_P10_GAS_MAX_YEAR__, when the coldest winter of the century met incomes little more than a third of today's in real terms; within the last fifty years the worst, __BURDEN_P10_GAS_MAX77_YEAR__ for gas and __BURDEN_P10_HP_MAX77_YEAR__ for electricity, came from the price of one fuel moving against the other. A home that can use either is less exposed to both. The 2023 English Housing Survey fuel poverty dataset shows who carries the exposure: households in the lowest tenth of after-housing-costs income have modelled heating costs (£__REV_FP_D1_HEAT_GBP__ a year for space heating and hot water) little below the top tenth's (£__REV_FP_D10_HEAT_GBP__) on a twelfth of the income, so heating takes a median __REV_FP_D1_HEAT_SHARE__% of theirs against __REV_FP_D10_HEAT_SHARE__%; __REV_FP_OVER10_PCT__% of all households are above 10%, and __REV_FP_D1_GAS_PCT__% of the poorest tenth heat with gas [@UKDS9466]. The households for whom the choice of fuel matters most are gas-heated now, and are not the ones best placed to operate a controller. That is a reason to think carefully before policy removes the choice from the homes least able to absorb a bad year, and a reason to make sure the controller that exercises the choice works for them.
