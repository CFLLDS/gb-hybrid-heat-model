## 7. Limitations

Every model is a simplification, and the conclusions of this paper are only as strong as the simplifications allow. The most important are set out here, roughly in order of how much they could change the answer. Where a sensitivity run tested a limit, its result is given.

### 7.1 Today's prices on yesterday's weather

The headline results price every weather year on the tariffs, grid carbon and national demand of 2025–26, matched by calendar day, with SVR, IOG and gas held at the October–December 2026 cap all year. This isolates the effect of weather, which is the point. The 86 winters are a stress set, not a forecast: how often a winter like 1962–63 occurred in the record is not the probability of one now, in a warmer climate (Section 5.1), and the results are conditional on today's prices and grid in each. It is also one price year, and the household answer turns on the ratio of electricity to gas prices more than on anything else. The matched-years run shows how much: at their own prices, the winters 2016–17 to 2024–25 give the hybrid __SENS_MATCHED_HYB_SVR_RANGE__% of homes on SVR, against __SENS_MATCHED_HYB_SVR_MAIN_RANGE__% at 2025–26 prices. Today's ratio is, in that respect, the least favourable to the hybrid of the past decade, and a future ratio could move the household results either way. The levy sweep (Section 5.1) shows the range.

Within a year, the calendar pairing does not know what prices *would* have been in a hard winter. A cold snap raises wholesale prices, so Agile would have been dearer on the coldest days of 1962–63 than on the same calendar days of 2025–26. The weather-matched run (Section 4.8) measures how much this matters for the __REV4_WM_YEARS__ test winters. It moves Agile, the one tariff that follows the market, a long way: the C-COP hybrid wins __REV4_WM_HYB_AGILE__% of homes on Agile in the median winter, against __REV4_WM_HYB_CAL_AGILE__% with the calendar pairing, and its carbon gap over an all-electric heat pump goes from __REV4_WM_KGGAP_CAL_AGILE__ to __REV4_WM_KGGAP_AGILE__ kg a year. On the fixed-price tariffs the share of homes the hybrid wins is unchanged (__REV4_WM_HYB_SVR__% on SVR, __REV4_WM_HYB_COSY__% on Cosy, __REV4_WM_HYB_IOG__% on IOG), and the carbon gap moves only through grid carbon, from __REV4_WM_KGGAP_CAL_SVR__ to __REV4_WM_KGGAP_SVR__ kg on SVR. The headline runs therefore understate the hybrid's cost advantage on a dynamic tariff, by enough to reverse the Agile answer for most homes, and understate the Agile herd (Section 5.4); the flat-tariff answers do not depend on the pairing. The matched run keeps 2025–26's levels, rescaled; it takes from 2016–26 only how the market moved with temperature, and that decade had no winter as cold as 1962–63, so for the hardest winters it too understates.

### 7.2 Every home a price-taker

The headline runs assume that what each home does has no effect on the prices it faces. That is reasonable for one home and unreasonable for __GB_HEATED_HOMES__: a herd of price-aware hybrids on Agile would raise the prices of the half-hours it crowds into. A sensitivity run adds this feedback (Section 4.8). The stock's extra demand in each half-hour raises that half-hour's Agile price, through the slope of wholesale price on national demand measured over 2016–26, and the stock is run again on the new prices, six passes in all. At the measured slope the added price averages __REV_FB1_ADD_MEAN_P__p/kWh and reaches __REV_FB1_ADD_MAX_P__p in the herd's half-hours, and the share of homes for which the hybrid is cheapest on Agile rises from __REV_FB1_HYB_AGILE_K0__% to __REV_FB1_HYB_AGILE__%, because dearer peaks send more heat to the boiler.

The herd shrinks but does not go. Across __REV_FB1_YEARS__ winters, the Agile hybrid's peak is still above SD-Power's cap in all but __REV_FB1_UNDER__ of __REV_FB1_RUNS__ passes, by up to __REV_FB1_WORST_GW__ GW, against __REV_FB1_HERD_K0_GW__ GW without feedback (8,000 homes per town). Only Agile is fed back; Cosy and IOG are rates a supplier sets, and their herds are untouched. The experiment has clear limits. It was not repeated at lower adoption, with homes spread across tariffs, or with the staggered response of Section 5.4, each of which would weaken the herd before any feedback. It is a damped fixed-slope iteration, a stand-in for a market rather than a model of one: real prices are set a day ahead from forecasts of the herd, suppliers may smooth or cap them, and controllers may learn. That it does not settle shows that this feedback moves demand between half-hours; it does not show where an actual market would settle, which could be well below the cap or above it. What it does show is that a herd of this size is not removed by a simple price response. The case for SD-Power never rested on the herd being as large as modelled; it rests on not having to rely on prices to undo a problem the prices created.

### 7.3 Thermal mass and pre-heating

The headline runs supply heat when the home needs it and store nothing in the building's fabric or in hot water ahead of a cheap window. Real heat pumps on time-of-use tariffs pre-heat and coast, as the case-study house does every night [@HPM454]. The pre-heating run measures what this leaves out: on IOG, pre-heating saves the all-electric home £__SENS_PRE_IOG_HP_SAVE__ a year and the hybrid £__SENS_PRE_IOG_CCOP_SAVE__ (on Cosy £__SENS_PRE_COSY_HP_SAVE__ and £__SENS_PRE_COSY_CCOP_SAVE__; on Agile £__SENS_PRE_AGILE_HP_SAVE__ and £__SENS_PRE_AGILE_CCOP_SAVE__). The all-electric home gains more, so the headline comparisons on time-of-use tariffs favour the hybrid more than a pre-heating home would. On SVR, a flat tariff, pre-heating gains nothing and the comparison is fair.

The pre-heating model is itself simple. Each home is a single thermal node with the SAP "medium" thermal mass, whatever its construction; each day is optimised separately, from the indoor temperature at midnight, with that day's prices known in advance; heat-pump efficiency is not reduced for the warmer water that storing heat needs; and the run used 8,000 homes per town, not 32,000. Measured cool-down rates in occupied homes vary widely [@Crawley2022], and the median time constant here, __STOCK_TAU_MEDIAN__ hours, is longer than the 31-hour national median one study derives from metered gas [@Halloran2024], so the pre-heating savings may be on the high side; it is not measured house by house. The first pre-heating run had no power limit and let homes store heat from direct electric heating at about 39 kW; it was discarded, the limit added and tested, and the run repeated (Section 4.10).

### 7.4 Weather at the town, not the house

ERA5 represents each town by a grid cell about 31 km across [@Hersbach2020]. Near the coast that cell includes sea air. Across the __TOWNS__ towns, ERA5's coldest 0.4% of hours over 1991–2020 is on average __ERA5_DESIGN_BIAS__ °C warmer than the station-based design temperatures used to size the heat pumps, and up to __ERA5_DESIGN_BIAS_MAX__ °C warmer at coastal towns. Over the 2025–26 heating season the archive also ran 0.4–0.9 °C warmer than the case-study house's own outdoor sensor. Two consequences follow. Heat pumps are sized for colder weather than the model then gives them, which is conservative for the all-electric case. And the coastal towns' weather is probably milder in the model than at the houses, which understates how often the hybrid wins there.

### 7.5 A real stock, calibrated on its mean

Each town's homes are drawn from its own dwelling records and calibrated to its metered gas, but with one factor per town: the calibration fixes each town's *mean* gas use, not its spread. Within a town, heat loss varies only with dwelling form and floor area, because certificate data say nothing about how a home is lived in, so the spread between homes is a lower bound and the share of homes on either side of a break-even is less certain than the town's typical home. Indoor temperature, gains and hot water are drawn independently of heat loss, although colder, leakier homes are in practice often kept cooler. The __STOCK_SCOT_PROXY_TOWNS__ Scottish towns borrow the North East of England's mix of homes, scaled to Scottish metered gas. Certificate demand is for gas-boiler homes only, so homes heated by oil, electricity or biomass are not in the stock.

Some inputs remain assumptions: the daily profile of internal gains, the 40–55 °C range of radiator design temperatures, and, most influential of all, each home's heat-pump efficiency level, drawn between the national field-trial median and the tuned systems on heatpumpmonitor.org. Three sensitivity runs test the level (Section 4.8), and it moves the household results more than any other input. With every home at the field-trial median, the hybrid is cheapest for __REV_COP_LO_HYB_SVR__% of homes on SVR and __REV_COP_LO_HYB_AGILE__% on Agile, and the all-electric home is still cheaper than a gas boiler for __REV_COP_LO_HP_BEATS_GAS_SVR__%. With every home as well tuned as the heatpumpmonitor.org fleet, the shares fall to __REV_COP_HI_HYB_SVR__% and __REV_COP_HI_HYB_AGILE__%, against __REV_COP_MID_HYB_SVR__% and __REV_COP_MID_HYB_AGILE__% in the main runs, and the heat pump is cheaper than gas for __REV_COP_HI_HP_BEATS_GAS_SVR__%. So the range of installations Britain actually has moves the household verdict a long way without reversing it on the flat and wholesale tariffs: a stock at the field-trial median makes the hybrid the cheaper choice for about a third of homes on SVR and Agile and two in three on Cosy, while a stock as well tuned as the heatpumpmonitor.org fleet makes the heat pump the cheaper choice for almost every home on SVR and Agile and most on Cosy (the hybrid wins for __REV_COP_HI_HYB_COSY__–__REV_COP_LO_HYB_COSY__% on Cosy across the two ends). Only on Cosy does the majority change sides between the ends; on IOG the hybrid stays ahead for most homes at both, __REV_COP_HI_HYB_IOG__–__REV_COP_LO_HYB_IOG__%. Giving leakier homes the poorer installations (a correlation of −0.5 between level and heat loss) barely moves the main-run shares, to __REV_COP_CORR_HYB_SVR__% on SVR and __REV_COP_CORR_HYB_AGILE__% on Agile. The carbon result changes in size but not in sign: at every level the hybrid emits more than the all-electric home, by __REV_COP_HI_KGGAP_SVR__–__REV_COP_LO_KGGAP_SVR__ kg a year on SVR. The commissioning that the Committee on Fuel Poverty found saves several hundred pounds a year [@Rivers2026CFP, p. 3] is therefore worth as much to the household verdict as any tariff. The trial's installers designed to a median flow temperature of __REV_EOH_DESIGN_FLOW_P50__ °C (10th to 90th percentile of __REV_EOH_DESIGN_FLOW_N__ homes __REV_EOH_DESIGN_FLOW_P10__–__REV_EOH_DESIGN_FLOW_P90__ °C) [@ESC2025_USmart], inside the model's range, and the pumps then ran at a median of __REV_EOH_FLOW_AT_0C_P50__ °C when delivering heat with 0 to 2 °C outside (__REV_EOH_FLOW_AT_0C_P10__–__REV_EOH_FLOW_AT_0C_P90__ °C across homes) [@UKDS9209]: the design range is a fair one, and weather compensation, which the model also applies, does the rest.

### 7.6 Gas meters are not gas-heated homes

National figures scale each region by its gas-heated homes: its domestic gas meters, __GB_GAS_METERS__ in 2024 [@DESNZ2025_MSOA_Gas], times __CAL_H_HEATED__, the midpoint of the census range of 0.81 homes heated by mains gas alone per domestic meter and 0.91 including homes that use gas alongside another fuel [@ONS2021_TS046]. The stock is calibrated on the same basis (Section 4.5), so Britain's total heat demand is fixed by metered gas whichever ratio is chosen: a lower ratio means fewer gas-heated homes, each leakier. The ratio still matters at the margin, through each home's heat-pump capacity and efficiency, but not to first order. An earlier draft calibrated per gas-heated home and scaled per meter, which counted each home's heat about 1/__CAL_H_HEATED__ times. No basis is a forecast of adoption: every national figure assumes *all* gas-heated homes adopt the controller in question, to show what each controller does to the system, not how many homes will choose it.

### 7.7 SD-Power, idealised

SD-Power dispatch is modelled as a perfect system operator: it knows Britain's total demand in every half-hour in advance, sheds exactly the share of homes needed, chosen at random, and every home responds. Its cap was set at the flat-tariff hybrid's own peak, not at a level any network operator has proposed, and on national demand; a cap on demand net of wind (Section 5.4) changes when homes are sent to gas, and what the cap protects. Real dispatch would face forecasting error, communications failures and households opting out, and would need a way to pay for the gas it calls on; the £__SDP_COST_AGILE_MEAN__ a year it costs the average home is borne by the household in the model, and no flexibility payment is counted against it. The model shows what coordinated dispatch can do to the peak and what it costs in fuel, not how it would be governed or paid for. Section 5.4 replays the dispatch with partial participation, forecast error, non-compliance, a one-half-hour lag and fixed caps; it shows the result survives the first three in moderation and fails under reactive dispatch. That replay has limits of its own: forecast errors are independent from one half-hour to the next, non-compliance is random, communications never fail as a block, and no home stores heat, so a home sent to gas does not draw extra power when it is released. A pre-heating stock would rebound, and that is not tested.

### 7.8 Historical prices and incomes

The burden analysis of Section 5.5 rests on a price series compiled for this paper. From 1948 it uses government and parliamentary figures; before 1948 prices are back-cast with general inflation and graded low confidence, and before 2010 the split between unit rates and standing charges uses the 2010–15 average. The income measures for the poorest tenth are the ONS series from 1977 and, from 1961 to 1976, the 10th percentile of equivalised disposable income computed from the Family Expenditure Survey microdata in the IFS Households Below Average Income dataset [@UKDS3300], spliced on their __REV_HBAI_OVERLAP_YEARS__ overlapping years (mean ratio __REV_HBAI_P10_RATIO__, never more than __REV_HBAI_P10_MAXDIFF_PCT__% apart); before 1961 only aggregate disposable income per household is available, built with an average household size held at its 1961 value. The 2022–23 burdens exclude the £400 Energy Bills Support Scheme credit. All burdens are for heating alone, for a typical home, and are not the official fuel-poverty measures.

### 7.9 What is not counted

The comparison is of running costs only, for a home that already has a working boiler and decides whether to keep it beside a new heat pump. Equipment, installation, maintenance and grants are excluded, so a new hybrid bought in place of a new heat pump, with a boiler to buy and later replace, is not assessed; so is the cost of keeping the gas network open for hybrid homes, which a full system comparison would have to include and which is significant [@Strbac2018]. Comfort is held as a floor in every case, so the model says nothing about homes that under-heat to save money, which are precisely the homes the fuel-poverty literature is most concerned with. The air-to-air results on the companion map use manufacturer efficiency, not measured data.

### 7.10 One controller rule, perfectly informed

The C-COP controller knows the current price and its heat pump's current efficiency exactly, and the pre-heating controller knows the whole day's prices in advance. Real controllers estimate both. The savings reported for software over a fixed switch are therefore an upper bound on what a real controller would capture, although the fixed-switch baselines are themselves generous: they assume an installer chose a sensible changeover temperature, and the Electrification of Heat trial found some hybrids with control faults that overused the boiler [@ESC2024].

### 7.11 The national peaks

Every national peak adds a stock's heat demand to Britain's demand in the same calendar half-hour of 2025–26. That baseline does not respond to a past winter's weather; moving it by each winter's temperatures (Section 5.4) raises the all-electric peak and the herd and lowers the flat-tariff hybrid's, but rests on a temperature response fitted to 2016–26, a range that does not reach the coldest winters of the record. The heat demand itself is the model's. Its after-diversity peak per home agrees with the Freedom report's estimate for a typical winter, but its homes are far more synchronised than real ones: a coincidence factor of __REV_CF34_CCOP_IOG_LO__ for 34 hybrid homes on a two-rate tariff against a measured 0.27 (Section 4.10). The herds on time-of-use tariffs are therefore a synchronised stress case. Habits measured in gas homes reproduce the measured diversity of gas heating and shrink the herd by about a fifth on Agile and Cosy and hardly on IOG; the habits of heat-pump homes shrink it not at all (Sections 4.10 and 5.4); and neither brings the model's heat pumps near the measured coincidence. Against the Electrification of Heat trial's own cold days (Table 3) the model's per-home coincident peak is about a fifth high on the main runs' assumptions and about right with measured habits, with individual peaks a third low and a poorly matched daily shape, so the national all-electric peaks carry an error of that order, and the shape of the day within them is the least trustworthy part. The main national figures assume that every gas-heated home adopts the controller in question; Table 9 gives the herd at lower adoption and mixed tariffs, where it is small or absent, so each full-stock peak is a conditional counterfactual for comparing controllers, not a forecast of Britain's maximum demand. Below the national level the feeder case (Table 10) is a thermal count on one town's homes with another city's base loads, without voltage, phase or cable models; a network study would need the operators' own feeder data.
